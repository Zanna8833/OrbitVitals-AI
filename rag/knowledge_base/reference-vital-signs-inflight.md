## How to use these values

There is no official NASA table of "normal in-flight vital signs". What exists are measured **changes from each crewmember's own pre-flight baseline** in small groups of astronauts (6-11 people per study). Therefore:

- Always compare a reading with the same crewmember's pre-flight values (same posture, same time of day) and with their own earlier in-flight values.
- Terrestrial reference ranges and hospital early-warning scores are Earth values; they are not validated in microgravity.
- A stable shift that matches the changes below is expected adaptation; a new trend away from the crewmember's in-flight plateau, or symptoms, is what matters.

## Blood pressure

- **24-hour ambulatory BP falls in long-duration flight.** In 8 ISS astronauts measured between flight days 85 and 192, 24-h average systolic BP was 8 mmHg lower, diastolic 9 mmHg lower and mean arterial pressure 10 mmHg lower than pre-flight [1].
- The normal night-time dip in BP (about 8 mmHg) is maintained in space [1].
- Mechanism: systemic vasodilation (vascular resistance down 39%), not reduced sympathetic activity [1].
- In a different ISS study (6 astronauts, 2-6 months) resting BP measured in brief sessions was not different from pre-flight [2]. So: a resting BP equal to or up to about 10 mmHg below the pre-flight baseline is consistent with published data.

Red flags: systolic/diastolic far below the personal in-flight trend with dizziness, confusion or fainting (dehydration, bleeding, sepsis, arrhythmia); a sustained rise above pre-flight values (not expected in flight).

## Heart rate and cardiac output

- **Resting heart rate is essentially unchanged** in flight compared with pre-flight [1][2].
- **Stroke volume rose 35% and cardiac output 41%** between 3 and 6 months on ISS [1]; one study found a late in-flight increase in cardiac output [2].
- Heart rate variability (high-frequency component) and pulse pressure were reduced in flight [2].
- After landing, seated resting HR was slightly higher than pre-flight (60 vs 55 beats/min) and baroreflex slope 34% lower during paced breathing [2].

Red flags: resting HR persistently above the crewmember's in-flight baseline (dehydration, fever, infection, anxiety, bleeding, arrhythmia), irregular pulse, chest pain, palpitations or syncope.

## Body temperature

- **Resting core body temperature rises by about 1 °C** during long-duration flight, developing gradually over about 2.5 months and reaching a plateau after about 75 days; it returns slowly to baseline after landing (11 ISS astronauts, forehead heat-flux sensor) [3].
- During exercise, maximal core temperature was on average 1.39 °C higher than on Earth and exceeded 40 °C in some individuals [3].

Implication: a resting core temperature around 38 °C after several months may be the crewmember's in-flight baseline, not fever. Terrestrial fever cut-offs (e.g. 37.5-38 °C) produce false alarms in flight. Fever should be judged against the individual's in-flight trend plus symptoms (chills, localized pain, cough, urinary symptoms, rash). Note that the ISS kit thermometer and a forehead heat-flux sensor measure different sites.

## Breathing, SpO2 and blood volume

- **Respiration**: on 9-14 day Spacelab flights tidal volume fell 15% and respiratory rate rose 9%, with unchanged alveolar ventilation, O2 uptake and end-tidal PO2 [5]. On ISS (130-196 days) vital capacity was essentially unchanged; O2 uptake and CO2 output were about 12% lower [4].
- **SpO2**: the ISS cabin is normoxic (14.7 psia, 21% O2, inspired PO2 about 150 mmHg, like sea level) [7]. A Shuttle study found in-flight SpO2 under mild hypoxia no different from pre-flight, i.e. microgravity did not change oxygen saturation [7]. No published in-flight SpO2 normal range was found; the crewmember's own pre-flight sea-level SpO2 is the reference. A drop is not explained by spaceflight adaptation and should prompt checks of cabin ppO2/pressure and of the crewmember.
- **Blood volume**: within about 2 days in microgravity, circulating (intravascular) volume falls by about 15% as fluid shifts headward and leaves the vessels [6]. This causes facial puffiness and leg volume loss and drives orthostatic intolerance on return [6].

## Escalation thresholds used on ISS

The ISS emergency checklist uses these bedside thresholds [8]:
- Assist breathing if respiratory rate is **below 12 or above 30 per minute**, if lips/face/hands are blue-grey, or if the patient is snoring or struggling to inhale.
- Pulse and respiratory rate are counted for 15 seconds and multiplied by four.

General rule for the assistant: contact the flight surgeon when a vital sign moves outside the crewmember's established in-flight range and is accompanied by symptoms, or when a trend continues over several days without explanation.
