## Why nutrition matters in space

NASA notes that inadequate food and nutrient intake in space has led to weight loss, bone and muscle loss, cardiovascular deconditioning and impaired immune function [1]. Weight loss occurred even on short Gemini, Apollo, Skylab, ASTP and ISS flights, partly because of food acceptability [1]. In the Mars-500 isolation study, food was likely the greatest problem [1]. Alcohol is prohibited on ISS because of its effects on the life support and waste systems [1].

## NASA requirements

- **Energy and nutrients**: each crewmember must receive 100% of their calculated nutrient and energy requirements, based on age, sex, body mass, height and activity factor (V1 4020) [5][1]. NASA uses the US Dietary Reference Intakes, with **vitamin D increased** to make up for the lack of sunlight; extra calories are added for EVAs [1].
- Pre-flight nutritional status is assessed and deficiencies corrected before launch (V1 4019) [5]; post-flight treatment aims to return to the pre-mission baseline (V1 4022) [5][1].
- **Water** (NASA-STD-3001 Vol 2, Table 6.3-1) [2]:

| Use | Minimum quantity |
|---|---|
| Drinking and food rehydration | 2.5 L per crewmember per day |
| EVA | 240 mL per EVA hour |
| Medical contingency | 5 L per event |
| Eye irrigation | 500 mL per crewmember |
| Re-entry fluid loading | 1 L per crewmember (with salt tablets) |
| Suited, launch/entry suit | 2 L per 24 h |

## What astronauts actually consumed

Average intake on past programs (mean values from NASA data) [1]:

| Program | Energy (kcal/day) | Energy (% of WHO estimate) | Water (g/day) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apollo | 1880 | 64% | 1647 |
| Shuttle | 2090 | 74% | 2223 |
| ISS (Expeditions 26-34) | 2444 | 84% | 2320 |

Astronauts have usually eaten **less energy than estimated** and many drank less than the 2.5 L allocation. Sodium intake on ISS was high (about 3800-4700 mg/day) [1].

## Dehydration

Losing water equal to 2.5% of body weight impairs decision-making and concentration and reduces physical performance potential by 35% [2].

Signs and symptoms [2]:
- reduced urine output;
- reduced physical performance;
- headache, feeling ill;
- difficulty concentrating, confusion;
- sleepiness;
- poor temperature regulation.

In the first days of flight, fluid shift causes temporary increased urine output, and space motion sickness with nausea, vomiting and poor appetite reduces intake, raising dehydration risk [4]. Some crew restrict drinking to avoid toilet use, which increases kidney stone risk [3].

## Links to other problems

- **Kidney stones**: low fluid intake plus high sodium and protein, combined with calcium released from bone, raise stone risk; hydration is a main countermeasure [3].
- **Bone and muscle**: adequate energy intake is part of what protects bone (see bone and muscle document).
- **Orthostatic intolerance** at landing is worsened by low fluid volume; fluid loading before re-entry is standard (see orthostatic intolerance document).

## Monitoring cues for the assistant

- Body mass trend versus the crewmember's own pre-flight and early in-flight values (see body mass reference).
- Logged food and water intake versus the individual's calculated requirement and the 2.5 L/day allocation.
- Reduced urine output, headache, sleepiness or rising heart rate versus baseline as possible dehydration signs; report to the flight surgeon if persistent or combined with vomiting or fever.
