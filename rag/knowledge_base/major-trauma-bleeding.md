## Why trauma is different in space

The risk of serious trauma in flight is low, but a major hemorrhage would be catastrophic because resources are very limited [1]. Key differences from Earth described in a 2022 review (Frontiers in Bioengineering and Biotechnology) [1]:

- **Reduced reserve**: after adaptation, circulating blood volume is reduced (about 10-23% in the studies reviewed) and red cell mass is reduced (about 10-20% versus pre-flight), so the injured crewmember has less volume to lose. The effect on red cells diminishes with longer missions.
- **Limited fluids and no blood**: on ISS the only resuscitation fluid is about 4 L of normal saline [1]; the 2015 ISS kit list shows 8 bags of 500 mL IV fluid and one IV pressure infusor [2]. Stored blood needs refrigeration and lasts about 35 days, so it is not carried [1].
- **Fluids behave differently**: without gravity, IV fluid does not flow out of a bag on its own (external pressure needed, risk of air bubbles), and infusion rate is hard to control [1].
- **Non-compressible torso bleeding** (chest, abdomen, pelvis) cannot be controlled by pressure and may be untreatable on board [1].
- Evidence is sparse: how hemorrhagic shock evolves in a space-adapted cardiovascular system is largely unknown [1].

## Immediate actions

1. **Scene safety first**: stop the hazard (moving equipment, electrical source, sharp debris); trigger the medical emergency call and get help.
2. **Control external bleeding**: direct pressure with gauze; for limb bleeding, tourniquets or hemostatic dressings may be adequate [1]. The 2015 ISS kit includes gauze 4x4, rolled gauze, wound packing and ACE bandages; the only tourniquet listed is in the IV supply pack [2].
3. **Contain blood**: in microgravity blood is not drained away by gravity; use gauze and absorbent material, gloves and eye protection, and bag contaminated material in biohazard bags (present in the kit) [2].
4. **Restrain patient and caregiver**: the review lists restraint of both the Crew Medical Officer and patient as a prerequisite for procedures [1]; use the Crew Medical Restraint System [3].
5. **Check airway and breathing**; if the patient is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR (see the cardiac arrest document).
6. **Call MCC-Houston** for a private medical conference with the flight surgeon as soon as the bleeding is controlled.

## IV access and fluids

- ISS CMOs are trained in IV insertion, endotracheal intubation and chest tube placement in addition to advanced trauma life support principles [1].
- Use a degassed infusion bag inside a pressure bag (pressure infusor) to give fluids; intraosseous access is the fallback if a vein cannot be found [3].
- Terrestrial remote damage-control resuscitation uses permissive hypotension (palpable radial pulse, normal mental status, systolic BP about 80-100 mmHg, higher for head injury) to limit bleeding while preserving organ perfusion [1]. This is a terrestrial austere-medicine concept; any target in flight is set by the flight surgeon.
- Prolonged fluid infusion can exhaust supplies, worsen clotting and cause hypothermia [1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon / emergency criteria

Contact immediately for any of:
- bleeding not controlled by direct pressure, or a tourniquet applied;
- signs of shock: rising heart rate compared with the crewmember's own in-flight baseline, falling blood pressure, pale/cold/sweaty skin, confusion;
- chest, abdominal or pelvic injury, head injury with any loss of consciousness, suspected fracture of a long bone or spine;
- shortness of breath after chest trauma (possible pneumothorax).

ISS policy for serious medical problems is stabilization and expeditious return to Earth; even from ISS an emergency return could take more than 24 hours [1].

## Exploration missions

On Moon or Mars missions return is not quick, communication may be delayed, and the crew must work autonomously [1]. Damage-control concepts (do only the essential life-saving steps), stored freeze-dried plasma, hemostatic agents and fresh whole blood from crewmates are discussed in the literature but are not established space protocols [1]. Partial-gravity surface operations will add injury risk that current estimates do not include [1].

## Follow-up and monitoring

- Repeated heart rate, blood pressure, SpO2, respiratory rate, mental status and wound checks; trend against the crewmember's in-flight baseline.
- Record estimated blood loss, fluids given, tourniquet time.
- Watch for delayed signs of internal bleeding: heart rate rising and blood pressure falling compared with the crewmember's in-flight baseline, pallor, sweating, confusion. In-flight blood pressure is already lower than on Earth (see vital signs reference), so terrestrial cut-offs can mislead.
- Photograph wounds for the surgeon (ISS checklists routinely use photo documentation and downlink) [2].
