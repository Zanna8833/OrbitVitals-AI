## Kit organization

The publicly released ISS medical checklist (2015 pages, obtained through FOIA) lists the kit as color-coded packs [1]:
- **Convenience Medication Pack (White)**: common over-the-counter type drugs.
- **Emergency Medical Treatment Pack (Red)**: emergency drugs (e.g. EpiPen) and basic airway equipment (bag-valve mask).
- **IV Supply Pack (Gray)**, **Medical Diagnostic Pack (Blue)** (blood oximeter, blood pressure monitor, eye chart), **Medical Supply Pack (Green)**, **Minor Treatment Pack (Pink)** (bandages, splints, sutures, skin stapler, surgical tools, dental tools).
- **Oral Medication Pack (Purple)**, **Topical & Injectable Medication Pack (Brown)**, **Physician Equipment Pack (Yellow)** (advanced airway hardware).

Each drug entry gives strength, route, quantity, possible side effects and comments such as daily maximums or "do not combine" warnings [1].

This is the 2015 list. Kits change over time and differ between vehicles; for example, NASA's 2026 venous thrombosis brief describes anticoagulant treatment with apixaban, which is not in the 2015 list [4][1]. Check the current onboard inventory.

## What the kit covers (examples from the 2015 list)

Drug groups in the kit [1]:
- **Pain and fever**: acetaminophen, ibuprofen, aspirin, ketorolac (IM), hydrocodone/acetaminophen, hydromorphone (IM), ketamine (IM).
- **Motion sickness and nausea**: promethazine (oral and IM), meclizine, ondansetron.
- **Sleep and alertness**: melatonin, zaleplon, zolpidem, modafinil, caffeine.
- **Allergy and congestion**: loratadine, fexofenadine, diphenhydramine, pseudoephedrine, oxymetazoline, mometasone, cromolyn, saline spray.
- **Antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals**: amoxicillin, azithromycin, doxycycline, levofloxacin, sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim, metronidazole, clindamycin, ceftriaxone and ertapenem (IM), valacyclovir, fluconazole, clotrimazole.
- **Emergency and cardiac**: epinephrine, nitroglycerin, albuterol, prednisone, dexamethasone.
- **Behavioral health**: sertraline, venlafaxine, lorazepam, aripiprazole (oral and IM).
- **Neurology**: diazepam (IM), phenytoin.
- **Gastrointestinal and urinary**: loperamide, bisacodyl, omeprazole, ranitidine, tamsulosin; urinary catheters.
- **Eye, skin and dental**: anesthetic, antibiotic and lubricating eye drops; topical steroids and antibiotics; temporary filling, dental adhesive, eugenol.

## How medications are used in flight

- About 71% of crew use sleep aids (about 10 times more than adult ambulatory care on Earth) and about 21% use alertness aids; sleep aids are the leading reason for acute medication use in flight [3].
- Head and nasal congestion is the leading cause of prolonged medication use [3].
- Crews perceived medications used in low Earth orbit as more than 80-90% effective, and 85% reported no side effects (Shuttle data) [2].
- Apart from sleep medication, overall use is similar to Earth [2].

## Do drugs keep in space?

- In a NASA study of nine medications flown on ISS for 550 days, all but melatonin stayed within US Pharmacopeia limits (90-110% of label content); degradation was less than 5-10% for the selected medications [2].
- Low Earth orbit experience has not shown changes in medication stability, dosing or effectiveness leading to poor health outcomes [2].
- Concern for exploration: missions of 1-5 years exceed typical expiration dates of 1-2 years, with no resupply and more radiation exposure; ISS drugs are restocked before they expire [2].

## Spaceflight cautions

- Absorption may change: motion sickness and its drugs alter gastric emptying; intestinal transit is faster and more variable [2].
- Avoid drugs that depress the heart or dilate vessels where possible, because all crew have cardiovascular deconditioning and fluid shifts [3].
- Sprays may be less reliable than tablets in microgravity and airflow [3].
- Drugs that raise urinary calcium increase kidney stone risk and load the water recycling system [3].
- Sedating drugs before critical tasks or emergencies can impair performance.

**Rule for the assistant**: name kit items only as information; doses and choices are per the flight surgeon and the onboard medical checklist.
