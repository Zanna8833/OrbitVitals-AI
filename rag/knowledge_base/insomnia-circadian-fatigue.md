## Recognition

- **Short or poor sleep**: difficulty falling or staying asleep, early waking, unrefreshing sleep. ISS astronauts averaged about 6.1 h per night by actigraphy [3]; NASA estimates an average in-flight deficit of about 0.3 h (18 minutes) per day compared with the pre-flight period [2]. Compare each crewmember with their own baseline (see sleep reference document).
- **Circadian desynchrony**: sleep and wake occurring at the wrong internal clock time, as in jet lag or shift work. Causes in flight: chronic sleep loss, schedule shifts, work during biological night, altered light cycles [1].
- **Fatigue effects**: inattention, distraction, slower reaction time, poor decisions, irritability, low mood [1][5]. After seven days of six hours of sleep, performance equals that of a person awake for 24-36 hours [2].

## Immediate actions (self-care first)

1. **Check the sleep environment**: NASA lists noise, temperature, light leaks, vibration, CO2, lack of privacy and sleep station location as sleep disruptors [2]. Check cabin ppCO2 near the sleep station (see CO2 document).
2. **Sleep hygiene** (NASA Fatigue Management Service best practices) [1]:
   - cool, dark, quiet sleep space, no screens;
   - avoid naps unless scheduled;
   - no caffeine 6-12 hours before sleep (individual);
   - within 2 hours of sleep avoid vigorous exercise and large or spicy meals.
3. **Time light exposure** [1]:
   - 1-2 hours before bed: avoid bright or blue-enriched light, cover windows, use eye masks or blue-blocking settings on devices;
   - 1-2 hours after waking: seek bright, blue-enriched light; continue light exposure through the day if fatigued.
4. **Report schedule problems** (shifts, late work) so the ground can adjust the timeline; NASA requires planned limits on work hours and sleep periods (V1 4014) [6][2].

## Medications

Only per flight surgeon or Fatigue Management Service guidance.
- NASA-approved categories [1]: chronobiotics to shift the clock (melatonin), hypnotics (melatonin; benzodiazepine receptor agonists such as zaleplon, half-life about 1 hour), and alertness drugs (caffeine, modafinil, armodafinil). NASA tests medications on the ground before flight so each astronaut knows their response [1].
- The 2015 ISS kit contains melatonin 3 mg, zaleplon 10 mg, zolpidem 10 mg and modafinil 200 mg tablets; the kit warns not to combine sleep aids with other sleep or sedating drugs [4].
- Hypnotic use is common: 75% of ISS crewmembers reported using a sleep aid [3].
- Caution: a sleep aid taken shortly before an emergency or critical task can impair performance.

## When to contact the flight surgeon

- Several consecutive nights clearly below the crewmember's own in-flight average, or rising sleep aid use (monitoring heuristic, not a NASA threshold).
- Fatigue before an EVA, docking, robotics or other critical task.
- Poor sleep combined with headaches (possible CO2), low mood, anxiety or withdrawal (see behavioral health document), or snoring and breathing pauses.
- Side effects of sleep or alertness drugs (sleepwalking, memory gaps, palpitations, anxiety).

Historical lesson: Gemini 11's first EVA was ended early because of astronaut fatigue [5].

## Follow-up

- Keep a sleep log or actigraphy, sleep aid use and subjective alertness.
- Compare with the crewmember's own baseline and with published ISS values (about 6.1 h per night) [3]; flag consecutive short nights before critical tasks.
- Discuss persistent problems at the private medical conference; the ground Fatigue Management Service can recommend a shifted schedule and countermeasures [1].
- Long-term circadian disruption is linked on Earth to cardiovascular, metabolic and other diseases [1].
