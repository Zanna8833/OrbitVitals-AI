## Recognition

A severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) is a rapid, whole-body reaction after exposure to a trigger (food, medication, chemicals, payload materials). Typical signs to look for:
- Skin: hives, flushing, swelling of lips, tongue, face or throat.
- Airway/breathing: hoarseness, throat tightness, wheeze, difficulty breathing, blue or grey lips.
- Circulation: dizziness, fainting, fast pulse, low blood pressure.
- Gut: cramps, vomiting.

The ISS Medical Checklist treats "severe allergic reaction" as an emergency procedure whose objective is rapid treatment [1]. Do not wait for all signs; breathing difficulty or collapse after a likely trigger is enough to act.

Space context: spaceflight causes immune dysregulation, and some crews experience persistent hypersensitivity reactions during flight, and it is not possible to predict which crewmember will be affected [3]. A crewmember with new allergic symptoms in flight should be reported even if mild.

## Immediate actions: EpiPen (ISS Medical Checklist 1.101)

From the ISS emergency procedure "1.101 Severe Allergic Reaction" (checklist version dated 2015, released by NASA under FOIA) [1]:

1. Get the **Epinephrine (EpiPen)** from the red Emergency Medical Treatment Pack. The kit list shows EpiPen auto-injectors of epinephrine 1:1000, 0.3 mL, intramuscular [1].
2. Remove the EpiPen from its protective plastic tube; form a fist around it with the thumb nearest the safety cap; pull off the safety cap [1].
3. Do not put fingers over the needle end; do not inject through clothing (needle contamination) [1].
4. Press the needle end perpendicular and firmly against the **outer thigh** with a jabbing motion until it clicks; **hold for 10 seconds** [1].
5. Put the used pen back in its tube, secure it with gray tape and discard [1].
6. **Call MCC-Houston to request a private medical conference (PMC)** [1].
7. The EpiPen **may be repeated every 20 minutes** if symptoms recur or progress [1].
8. Continue with the checklist's allergic reaction procedure (2.0.403) [1].

## In-flight specifics and supportive care

- Restrain the crewmember (seat or Crew Medical Restraint System) so you can monitor them and have hands free.
- If breathing is difficult, follow the ISS "Difficulty Breathing" procedure: count pulse and respiratory rate, give oxygen by low-flow non-rebreather mask from the Respiratory Support Pack, give inhaled albuterol (two puffs), apply the pulse oximeter, listen to both lungs, measure blood pressure, then call MCC-H [1].
- If the crewmember stops breathing normally or loses pulse, start CPR (see the cardiac arrest document) [1].
- Other medicines carried in the 2015 ISS kit that a flight surgeon may direct after epinephrine include antihistamines (diphenhydramine oral and intramuscular; fexofenadine, loratadine), steroids (prednisone, methylprednisolone, intramuscular dexamethasone) and IV fluids [1]. Doses are directed by the flight surgeon / checklist; this document does not replace them.
- Medication stability: NASA reports that medications on ISS have generally remained usable and are restocked before expiration, but long exploration missions will exceed typical 1-2 year expiration dates [2]. Check the date of the EpiPen during routine kit audits, and use it anyway in an emergency if no other is available, while informing the surgeon.

## When to contact the flight surgeon

- Always, immediately after giving epinephrine [1].
- Urgently if symptoms return after the first dose, if a second dose is needed, or if there is any airway swelling, low blood pressure, or decreased consciousness.
- On deep-space missions with communication delay, give epinephrine first and report afterwards; do not delay treatment waiting for a reply.

## Follow-up and monitoring

- Monitor pulse, respiratory rate, blood pressure and SpO2 repeatedly for several hours; compare with the crewmember's own in-flight baseline (see the vital signs reference document).
- Watch for a second wave of symptoms after initial improvement.
- Identify and isolate the trigger (food item, medication, chemical) and record it; inform the crew to avoid it.
- Record times of exposure, symptoms, each epinephrine dose and other drugs for the surgeon.
- Replace used EpiPens in the inventory log; the 2015 kit list shows 3 EpiPens [1].
