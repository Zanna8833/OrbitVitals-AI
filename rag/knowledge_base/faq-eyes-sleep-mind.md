## Q: My near vision has got worse over two weeks. Is this SANS?

It may be. SANS (spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome) typically causes a far-sighted shift that blurs near vision over weeks to months. NASA reports that about 70% of long-duration crew show at least one SANS sign and about 18% have clinically concerning SANS [1].

What to do:
- Switch to your next stronger pair of glasses; all crew fly with several pairs of different power for this [1].
- Report the change at your next private medical conference; the surgeon may move up your in-flight eye exam (OCT, fundoscopy, ultrasound) [1].

Contact the flight surgeon sooner for sudden vision loss, double vision, eye pain, a new severe headache, or vision that does not improve with the stronger glasses. To date, vision after flight has always been correctable with glasses [1]. See the SANS document.

## Q: Something got into my eye. What should I do?

From the ISS toxic exposure procedure [2]:
1. Remove and discard contact lenses.
2. Flush the eye immediately with water from a drink bag, then use the eyewash station until the pain resolves or the surgeon tells you to stop.
3. If a chemical caused it, the rescuer must protect their own eyes, airway and skin first.
4. Request a private medical conference, and photograph the eye for the surgeon.

NASA reserves at least 500 mL of water per crewmember for eye irrigation [3]. Contact the flight surgeon immediately for any chemical in the eye, reduced vision, an object stuck in the eye, or severe pain. See the eye document.

## Q: I slept only 4 hours for three nights. Should I take a sleep medication?

Short sleep is common in space. ISS astronauts averaged about 6.1 hours, and 75% reported using a sleep aid at least once [4]. Three nights of 4 hours is well below that, so act on it.

First:
- Check your sleep station for noise, light, temperature and airflow/CO2.
- Follow NASA sleep hygiene: no caffeine 6-12 hours before sleep, avoid bright or blue light 1-2 hours before bed, and get bright light after waking [5].
- Ask the ground to review your schedule.

Medication: the ISS kit contains melatonin, zaleplon and zolpidem, with a warning not to combine them with other sedating drugs [2]. Use one only after discussing it with the flight surgeon, who knows how you responded in ground testing [5]. Keep in mind that a sleep aid may leave you less alert if an alarm wakes you.

See the insomnia and fatigue document.

## Q: I feel low and irritable and I'm avoiding the crew. What can I do?

This is a recognized spaceflight problem. Stress, depression, anxiety, relationship problems and grief are the most common behavioral concerns in flight [6]. What to do:
- Rule out physical contributors: poor sleep, high CO2, low oxygen, medication side effects, illness [6].
- Ask for a Private Psychological Conference. It is confidential and is a routine part of the mission [6].
- Use the support available: family conferences, care packages, personal time, journaling and Earth photography [6].
- Talk with the ground about your workload.

Contact the flight surgeon promptly if low mood lasts more than a few days, if you can't sleep, or if you cannot do your work. Contact them immediately if you have any thoughts of harming yourself or anyone else. See the behavioral health document.
