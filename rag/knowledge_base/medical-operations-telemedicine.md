## The medical team

From NASA's medical operations brief [1]:
- **Flight Surgeon (FS)**: a physician certified in aerospace medicine.
- **Crew Surgeon and Deputy Crew Surgeon**: chosen for each increment from the flight surgeon group. They manage everything medical from pre-flight to after landing: care, countermeasures, biomedical data, flight rules, and access to medical, dental and behavioral health specialists.
- **Biomedical Engineer (BME)**: technical and operational support to the surgeons, daily status reports, coordinates private medical, psychological and family conferences.
- **Crew Medical Officer (CMO)**: a crewmember with extra medical training who must be proficient in all procedures in the ISS medical checklist, performs in-flight physicals and manages onboard medical data. **Two CMOs are designated for each increment**.
- **Astronaut Strength, Conditioning and Rehabilitation specialists (ASCR)**: prescribe individual exercise.

CMO advanced training covers advanced life support, ambulatory care, emergency care, medical hardware, in-flight exams, checklist procedures, medical records, behavioral health, and telemedicine [1].

## Private Medical Conference (PMC) schedule

The ISS program schedules PMCs (15 minutes per crewmember) [2]:
- daily for the first five days after docking;
- weekly after that;
- before each EVA (within 24 hours of suit donning) and after each EVA (within 24 hours of suit removal);
- daily starting five days before entry, and on the morning of entry/landing;
- **at any time** if requested by the Flight Director, the commander, any crewmember or the crew surgeon.

PMCs must be held in a private location (audio and video) with a way to break in for emergencies [1]. Private Psychological Conferences (PPCs) are also scheduled [1].

## Onboard capabilities

NASA lists capabilities a mission may need [3]:
- give and manage medications;
- measure and store vital signs (blood pressure, oxygen saturation, heart rate, respiratory rate);
- physical exam and procedures (e.g. catheter, suturing, blood draw);
- behavioral and cognitive assessments, sleep/fatigue monitoring, diet monitoring;
- private two-way audio and video with the ground;
- knowledge support such as computer-based therapy, smart medical devices and just-in-time training.

Scheduled in-flight evaluations listed in NASA's medical operations brief (2025) include private medical and psychological conferences, hearing assessments, periodic health status checks, periodic fitness exams, pre- and post-EVA examinations and pre-landing medical evaluations [1]. See the in-flight monitoring schedule reference for timings.

Imaging examples: in-flight ultrasound and OCT for the eyes [4]; guided ultrasound software (AMOS) to let non-physician astronauts scan for kidney stones [6].

## Tempus Pro telemedicine device (research study)

NASA HRP lists Tempus Pro as a past investigation that tested a commercial telemedicine device for collecting and integrating vital health data, providing medical imaging and supporting near-real-time diagnosis [9]. Its data were compared with standard periodic health exams after the mission. During participating missions the crew medical officer also made a diagnosis for a simulated medical scenario using device data, input from a crew member acting as the patient and supplementary information [9]. The page says results may help inform future protocols; it gives no results. Tempus Pro is a test of a possible future capability, not part of the standard ISS medical kit.

## How to reach the ground in a medical problem

- Emergency checklist procedures repeatedly instruct: **"Call MCC-H to request PMC"** after the first life-saving steps (for example after giving epinephrine, or during CPR) [5].
- In a true emergency, life-saving steps come first; the ground is informed as soon as a crewmember is free.
- For non-urgent issues, bring them to the next scheduled PMC or request one.
- All medical data from every mission phase goes into the crewmember's electronic medical record [1].


Notes for the assistant:
- Recommend "contact the flight surgeon / request a PMC" rather than giving a diagnosis.
- Use the PMC schedule to time reminders (e.g. pre-EVA check, pre-entry days).
- Respect privacy: medical conversations are private between crewmember and surgeon [1].

## When the ground decides on an early return: Crew-11 (2026)

In January 2026 an ISS crew returned to Earth earlier than planned because of a medical concern with one crewmember [7][8]:
- On 7 January 2026 a NASA astronaut (later identified by NASA as Mike Fincke) had a medical event that needed immediate attention. Crewmates responded quickly and, with guidance from NASA flight surgeons, his condition stabilized quickly [8].
- NASA described the return as "not an emergency" but a coordinated plan to use advanced medical imaging not available on the station [8]. The whole Crew-11 crew (four people) splashed down off San Diego on 15 January 2026 after 167 days in space, earlier than planned, and had a planned overnight hospital evaluation [7].
- NASA did not release the diagnosis, citing medical privacy [7].

Lessons for the assistant: the ISS can stabilize a crewmember with crew first aid plus real-time flight surgeon guidance, but diagnosis may still require tools only available on Earth (for example advanced imaging). The decision to return early is made by the ground; on a Moon or Mars mission this option is slower or absent (see the exploration autonomy document).
