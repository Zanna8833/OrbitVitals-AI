## Recognition

- Shortly after reaching microgravity, body fluids shift toward the head, causing congestion of the head and nose. Facial and periorbital (around the eyes) swelling is most evident in the first 3 days [2].
- Crewmembers describe facial or head fullness and discomfort in the head, sinuses and nose; congestion lasts from a few hours to a few days and then settles to a tolerable level as fluids readjust [2].
- NASA classes head and nasal congestion as space adaptation **or CO2-related**. It affects more than 50% of crewmembers and is the **leading cause of prolonged medication use in flight** [1].
- Congestion is part of space adaptation syndrome together with headache, back pain, drowsiness, constipation and urinary retention [2].

Newer data (2025): a review of medical records of 71 ISS astronauts (Expeditions 1-62, 2000-2019) found that 60 (85%) had at least one sinonasal medical event, 53 (75%) had general nasal congestion logged and 34 (48%) had events logged specifically as fluid-shift symptoms [5]. Of 754 logged events, 597 were general nasal congestion; ear clearing and barotrauma accounted for 17 events (first event in 7 astronauts, 10%), and most events occurred in the first flight week (107 events) [5]. This updates the older NASA figure of "more than 50%" [1]: both say congestion is very common; the 2025 study is the more recent and more specific count.

This is expected adaptation in the first days, not an illness.

## Immediate actions

1. **Check the cabin CO2** if congestion appears or worsens later in the mission, especially with headache (see CO2 headache document). NASA lists CO2 as a possible cause of congestion [1], and elevated CO2 causes headache [4].
2. **Saline nasal spray** first; the 2015 ISS kit contains saline nasal spray [3].
3. **Decongestants and allergy medication** per flight surgeon guidance. NASA lists pseudoephedrine (congestion or allergy medications) as treatment [2]. The 2015 ISS kit contains [3]:
   - pseudoephedrine 30 mg and 120 mg tablets;
   - oxymetazoline 0.05% nasal spray, with the warning **not to use for more than 3 consecutive days** because of rebound congestion;
   - mometasone nasal spray (steroid);
   - oral antihistamines (loratadine, fexofenadine).
4. Possible side effects to report: pseudoephedrine can raise blood pressure and cause restlessness, tremor and headache [3].

## In-flight specifics

- In the 2025 ISS study, pseudoephedrine was the most used medication for sinonasal symptoms (95 recorded uses; 73 for congestion, 20 as prophylaxis before EVA), followed by oxymetazoline spray (51 uses; 25 for congestion, 26 before EVA) [5]. Oxymetazoline use had a median of 1 day (mean 2.7) but a longest record of 29 days, so rebound congestion is possible [5].
- Crewmembers whose records mention EVA had on average about 9 more sinonasal records than those without, so EVA (suit pressure changes and ear clearing) is an occupational risk factor [5]. Ear pain or blocked ears after a pressure change: see the ear and sinus barotrauma document.
- Sprays may work less predictably than tablets in microgravity because of the lack of gravity and cabin airflow; NASA notes this as a pharmaceutical consideration for space [1].
- Later in flight, exercise and lower body negative pressure are used to counteract fluid-shift effects [2].
- Congestion can be confused with an upper respiratory infection. NASA's pre-flight Health Stabilization Program (quarantine, vaccinations) reduces the risk of infections in flight [1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

- Congestion with fever, facial pain over the sinuses, colored discharge or ear pain (possible infection).
- Need for decongestant spray beyond 3 days, or blood pressure rising above the crewmember's in-flight baseline while taking pseudoephedrine.
- New congestion together with headache in several crewmembers at once (check CO2 and the atmosphere, see cabin atmosphere reference).
- Nosebleed that does not stop with pressure (the kit contains nasal packing [3]).

## Follow-up

- Log symptom days and medication use; persistent congestion is the most common reason for long-term medication use [1].
- Compare with ppCO2 trend and sleep quality.
- Expect improvement over the first days as body fluids readjust [2].
- Count consecutive days of oxymetazoline (limit 3 days in the kit list) and check blood pressure against the in-flight baseline while taking pseudoephedrine [3].
