## What happens to neck veins in microgravity

The headward fluid shift distends the internal jugular veins (IJV). In 11 ISS crewmembers, IJV cross-sectional area rose from about 10 mm2 (pre-flight, seated) to about 70 mm2 in flight, and IJV pressure from about 5 to about 21 mmHg [2]. At about flight day 50, **6 of 11 (55%) had stagnant or reversed flow** in the IJV; **one had an occlusive thrombus** and a possible partial thrombus was found retrospectively in another [2]. Lower body negative pressure improved flow in 10 of 17 sessions [2].

NASA describes left IJV flow in flight as antegrade but slower than on Earth, stagnant, and/or retrograde; obstructive thrombi have been found in a very small number of crewmembers and were treated successfully with oral anticoagulants [1]. The first case (Auñón-Chancellor, NEJM 2020) led to on-orbit IJV surveillance of all NASA astronauts, now extended to all ISS crew [1].

## Recognition and risk factors

Most cases were found by ultrasound surveillance, not by symptoms. NASA asks that these be considered in the risk assessment [1]:
- IJV swelling; facial edema beyond normal space adaptation; eyelid edema; pupil dilation; headache; SANS symptoms.

Risk factors in the NASA algorithm [1]:
- Family history of unprovoked thrombosis in a first-degree relative (up to 2-fold; multiple relatives up to 4-fold).
- Thrombophilia (e.g. Factor V Leiden heterozygous about 4.9-fold).
- Estrogen-containing combined oral contraceptives (up to 3.2-fold), often used to suppress menstruation in flight; progestin-only options (IUD, implant) carry lower VTE risk.
- Stagnant flow on ultrasound is considered the most concerning pre-thrombotic sign; retrograde flow is a concern mainly if a clot forms [1].

## Surveillance schedule

For crew on missions over 30 days, IJV screening for thrombosis and flow anomalies is done pre-flight (L-12 to L-3 months), in flight at about **L+30 and L+60 days** and **R-42 days**, and after return, plus whenever clinically indicated [1]. In-flight ultrasound is performed by crew with real-time remote guidance from the ground. Findings are described as forward (antegrade but slower), stagnant or reversed (retrograde) flow; stagnant flow is the most concerning pre-thrombotic sign [1].

## NASA management algorithm

Per the NASA Medical Technical Brief (based on Bostick et al. 2025), applied by the flight surgeon [1]:

**Thrombus found on ultrasound:**
- Anticoagulate regardless of flow status. Apixaban (carried in the in-flight kit) has been used successfully: **10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice daily** [1].
- No EVA and no ARED resistive exercise for at least 30 days; treadmill (T2) and cycle (CEVIS) may be considered with harness shoulder straps removed [1].
- If clinically stable, generally remain on orbit at least 30 days to complete uninterrupted therapy [1].
- A crewmember on an estrogen contraceptive should not stop it when starting anticoagulation, because of the risk of abnormal uterine bleeding [1].

**No thrombus but stagnant flow:**
- With family history, thrombophilia or VTE-raising hormones: **prophylaxis with apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily** [1].
- Without those risk factors: more frequent monitoring and ensured hydration [1].

## Ongoing HRP research on venous blood flow

NASA HRP, jointly with the European Space Agency, lists the Venous Haemostasis study of physical differences in astronauts that may contribute to changes in venous blood flow and clot risk; scientists do not yet fully understand why flow problems occur in only some astronauts [3]. Design: preflight and postflight MRI to look for clots and vein structure; jugular vein ultrasound, blood draws and blood pressure checks before and during flight; and a test of whether the thigh cuffs used in other ISS studies mitigate abnormal flow [3]. It is a study, with no results on the page and no change to the surveillance or treatment above.

## Landing, bleeding and red flags

- Apixaban half-life is about 12 hours. Stop a **treatment dose at least 48 hours** before landing and a **prophylactic dose at least 24 hours** before landing, because of landing trauma risk; reversal agents are hard to give in space [1].
- If bleeding/trauma occurs while anticoagulated: compress the wound and skip the next doses until it is controlled, per flight surgeon [1].
- NASA recommends carrying tranexamic acid (heavy menstrual bleeding) and considering a thrombolytic for pulmonary embolism [1].

Contact the flight surgeon urgently for: sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing blood or fainting (possible pulmonary embolism); new one-sided neck swelling or pain; severe headache or neurological symptoms; any bleeding while on anticoagulation.

## Follow-up and monitoring

- Repeat ultrasound per the flight surgeon; record flow pattern (forward, stagnant, reversed) and clot size.
- Before landing, NASA guidance stops apixaban at least 48 hours ahead at treatment dose (24 hours at prophylactic dose) [1]; confirm timing with the flight surgeon.
- Track hydration and heart rate, and symptoms above. These guidelines are case-by-case; treatment is adjusted by the flight surgeon [1].
