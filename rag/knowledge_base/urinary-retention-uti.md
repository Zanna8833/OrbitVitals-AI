## Why urinary problems matter in space

According to NASA's Integrated Medical Model predictions for ISS, kidney stone is the second most likely reason for emergency medical evacuation and sepsis (driven mainly by urosepsis) the third [1]. NASA's urinary health brief groups three problems: urinary retention, urinary tract infection and kidney stones; kidney stones are covered in a separate document. Retention leads to infection, kidney infection (pyelonephritis) and potentially sepsis if untreated [1].

## Urinary retention: recognition and causes

Urinary retention is the impaired ability to urinate: hesitancy, difficulty starting, bladder fullness or pressure [1].
- **9 in-flight cases** documented (16 if suggestive symptoms are included); retention also happens on landing day [1].
- Odds of in-flight retention are **4.5 times higher in female astronauts**, which NASA notes is very different from terrestrial data [1].
- Causes: altered gravity vector (urine adheres to the bladder wall, so the urge to void may come only when the bladder is completely full; residual urine after voiding), psychosocial reluctance to use the toilet or the Maximum Absorbency Garment (MAG) especially early in flight, anticholinergic drugs used for space motion sickness (e.g. promethazine, meclizine, diphenhydramine list urinary retention as a side effect), infection, enlarged prostate [1][2].
- Fluid shift early in flight causes temporary diuresis, then altered bladder sensation and dehydration in the first days [3].

## Urinary retention: immediate actions

1. Try privacy, time and relaxation; drink fluids normally (do not restrict fluids to avoid toileting) [1].
2. Review medications taken (anticholinergic antiemetics or antihistamines) and report them [1].
3. Contact the flight surgeon (private medical conference). Options NASA lists are **catheterization** and **tamsulosin**, which increases urine flow [1]. The 2015 ISS kit contains Foley, coude, straight and short urinary catheters, a urine collection bag, 2% lidocaine jelly and tamsulosin 0.4 mg capsules [2].
4. Catheterization needs training, disinfection swabs, lubricant, catheters, collection devices and privacy; restraining the crewmember is harder in microgravity and the procedure takes time [1].
5. Percutaneous (suprapubic) bladder drainage under ultrasound guidance has been shown feasible in weightlessness but has not been used long-term [1].

Note: catheter use increases UTI risk; once an indwelling catheter is placed, the daily incidence of bacteria in the urine is 3-10% [1].

## Urinary tract infection: recognition

Symptoms to report: burning on urination, frequency, urgency, lower abdominal pain, cloudy or bloody urine; fever, chills, flank pain or nausea suggest kidney infection. Space risk factors: MAG use (urine splash-back, especially in women), residual urine, catheter use, toileting hygiene in a confined space, altered immunity [1]. The ISS kit includes urine Chemstrip test strips and a urine color chart [2].

Remember that resting core temperature runs about 1 °C higher after months in flight (see vital signs reference); compare temperature with the crewmember's in-flight baseline.

## UTI: treatment and escalation

- Antibiotics are the treatment of choice; prophylactic antibiotics may be considered in susceptible crew or with catheter use; hydration and analgesics help [1]. The 2015 ISS kit contains several oral antibiotics (e.g. sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, levofloxacin, amoxicillin) and injectable ceftriaxone and ertapenem [2]. Choice and dose: flight surgeon.
- Space challenges: limited antibiotic supply, shelf life, possible resistance, altered immune function [1].
- Historical case: Apollo 13's Fred Haise developed a serious urinary infection with fever, possibly catheter-related, made worse by cold, dehydration and stress; it was treated after landing [1].

Contact the flight surgeon **urgently** for: fever with flank pain, shaking chills, vomiting, confusion, low blood pressure or rising heart rate versus baseline (possible pyelonephritis or urosepsis), or inability to pass urine despite measures above.

## Follow-up and monitoring

- Fluid intake (target at least the 2.5 L/day NASA water allocation for drinking and food) and urine output.
- Urine dipstick results, temperature trend vs in-flight baseline, symptom log.
- After landing, retention risk is increased on post-flight day 1 [1].
- Record anticholinergic medications taken (for example for motion sickness), which can cause retention [1][2].
- Note catheter use and duration, since catheters raise infection risk [1].
