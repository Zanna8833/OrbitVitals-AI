## Recognition

Symptoms a crewmember or crewmate may notice: chest pain or pressure, palpitations (racing, pounding or skipped beats), an irregular pulse, breathlessness, dizziness, or fainting.

What is known about the heart rhythm in astronauts:
- **Atrial fibrillation (AF)** prevalence in active astronauts is about 5%, similar to the general population but at a younger age [1]. A 2010 NASA expert summit counted 11 atrial arrhythmias among active NASA astronauts since 1959 (7 AF, 1 atrial flutter, 3 supraventricular tachycardia), mostly paroxysmal and without symptoms; 5 of these crewmembers had catheter ablation before long-duration flight [2].
- In 13 astronauts studied before, during and after 6 months on ISS, left atrial volume rose transiently (by 12 mL on average) and some atrial ECG measures changed, but no AF episode was recorded [1].
- Historically, three space station evacuations in the 1970s and 1980s were due to suspected appendicitis, prostatitis and dysrhythmias [5].

Expected in flight: resting heart rate close to the pre-flight baseline (see the in-flight vital signs reference). A new irregular rhythm or a sustained rise above the crewmember's own in-flight baseline is not expected adaptation.

## Causes to rule out in flight

Chest pain or palpitations in a spacecraft can have space-specific causes:
- **After EVA or a cabin pressure drop**: decompression sickness with cardiopulmonary symptoms (dry cough, chest pain behind the breastbone, shortness of breath) or arterial gas embolism, which can cause cardiac ischemia; a patent foramen ovale raises the risk [4]. See the decompression sickness document.
- **Clot**: venous clots can travel to the lungs (pulmonary embolism) [4]; jugular vein clots have been found on ISS. See the venous thrombosis document.
- **After a rapid pressure change**: lung barotrauma can cause a collapsed lung (pneumothorax) or arterial gas embolism [6].
- **Triggers** of paroxysmal AF in young people: caffeine, sleep apnea and medications [2].
- **Heart attack or new AF** are considered unlikely in NASA-selected crews; NASA notes that programs not using NASA-level selection may need in-mission treatment for myocardial infarction and AF [3]. This matters for commercial crews.

## Immediate actions

General first steps (no public NASA chest pain checklist was found; follow the onboard medical checklist and the flight surgeon):
1. Stop all activity and restrain the crewmember at rest.
2. If the crewmember becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, start CPR (see the cardiac arrest document).
3. Measure heart rate (and note whether the pulse is regular), blood pressure and SpO2, and record the ECG if a cardiac monitor is available; NASA lists cardiac waveform monitoring among in-mission capabilities [3].
4. Note the time of onset, what the crewmember was doing, and any recent EVA, prebreathe, pressure change, new medication, caffeine or stimulant.
5. Request an urgent private medical conference; give medications from the kit (see the ISS medical kit document) only as directed by the flight surgeon or the onboard advanced life support procedure.
6. Keep a crewmate with the patient and repeat vital signs as directed by the flight surgeon.

## In-flight specifics

- Two views on whether spaceflight itself raises AF risk: the 2010 NASA summit found no evidence that any spaceflight factor increases atrial arrhythmias [2]; the 2018 imaging study concluded that 6 months of flight may cause transient atrial changes that could increase AF risk, although no AF occurred [1]. The 2018 study is more recent and based on direct measurements; both agree that AF has not been shown to be more frequent in flight.
- The 2010 summit recommended lowering dietary salt to under 5 g/day, noting in-flight sodium intake of 5-10 g/day [2].
- Anticoagulant or antiplatelet treatment (for example for AF or a clot) increases bleeding risk during EVA, and NSAIDs or aspirin taken for pain add to it [4].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Immediately (urgent PMC, inform the commander) for:
- chest pain at rest, chest pain after an EVA or pressure change, or chest pain with breathlessness;
- fainting or near-fainting, confusion, or new weakness or speech problems;
- an irregular pulse that does not settle, or a heart rate persistently far above the crewmember's in-flight baseline at rest;
- falling blood pressure or SpO2 compared with the in-flight baseline.

At the next scheduled PMC: occasional skipped beats without other symptoms, palpitations linked to caffeine, or short episodes that resolved.

## Follow-up and data to monitor

- Resting heart rate and rhythm trend against the pre-flight and in-flight baseline; episodes of palpitations with time and activity.
- Blood pressure and SpO2 trend; any ECG recordings sent to the ground.
- Caffeine, stimulant and decongestant use (pseudoephedrine can raise heart rate and blood pressure; see the nasal congestion document).
- Fluid and salt intake, since salt sensitivity and atrial stretch are possible contributors [2].
- For a crewmember with known AF or a treated PFO, EVA plans are a flight surgeon decision [4].
