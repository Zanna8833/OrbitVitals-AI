## Q: My resting temperature is 37.9 °C three months into the mission. Is this a fever?

Not necessarily. In 11 ISS astronauts, resting core body temperature rose by about 1 °C during long-duration flight, building up over about 2.5 months and reaching a plateau after about 75 days; during exercise core temperature was on average 1.39 °C higher than on Earth [1]. So a value near 38 °C after three months may be your in-flight baseline.

What matters is the change from your own in-flight trend and whether you have other symptoms: chills, sore throat, cough, burning on urination, flank pain, a rash or localized pain. Note which device you used; different thermometers measure different body sites.

Contact the flight surgeon if your temperature is clearly above your usual in-flight value, or if you have any of those symptoms. See the vital signs reference and the immune document.

## Q: My blood pressure is lower than it was on Earth. Is that a problem?

Usually not. In long-duration ISS flight, 24-hour blood pressure fell by about 8-10 mmHg as blood vessels relaxed, while heart rate was unchanged, stroke volume rose 35% and cardiac output rose 41% [2]. These are expected adaptations.

Compare your readings with your own early in-flight values. Contact the flight surgeon if your blood pressure drops further from your in-flight baseline, especially together with a faster heart rate, dizziness, vomiting, bleeding, fever or reduced urine output, which can point to dehydration, infection or blood loss. See the vital signs reference.

## Q: I get a headache every evening in my sleep station. Could it be CO2?

Possibly. CO2 builds up in a closed cabin, and ppCO2 varies slightly across ISS modules depending on where the scrubbers are and how air moves [3]. NASA limits the 1-hour average ppCO2 to no more than 3 mmHg to keep headache incidence below 1.4% [3]. Elevated CO2 causes headache, breathlessness and fatigue [3].

What to do:
- Check the ppCO2 reading for your module and the trend over the last days.
- Improve airflow around your head in the sleep station (check that the fan or vent is working).
- Ask whether other crewmembers have headaches; several at once points to the atmosphere.
- Report to the ground so the flight control team can act on CO2.

Fluid shift, poor sleep and dehydration also cause headaches. Seek the flight surgeon for a sudden severe headache, a headache with vision change, weakness or confusion, or one that is different from your usual. See the CO2 headache document.

## Q: What is my radiation dose and what is my limit?

Your dose comes from your personal dosimeter and the ground radiation team's analysis. Ask the ground for your current mission and career totals.

NASA limits [4]:
- Career: less than 600 mSv effective dose (for all ages and sexes).
- Solar particle events: the design reference event must give less than 250 mSv.
- All exposures are kept "as low as reasonably achievable".

Dosimeters measure absorbed dose (mGy), while limits are in effective dose (mSv), so do not compare the two numbers directly [5]. A sudden rise in dose rate during a solar event is the signal to take shelter. See the radiation dose reference and solar particle event documents.
