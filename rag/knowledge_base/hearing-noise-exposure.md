## Why noise matters in flight

Noise is the primary cause of hearing loss during spaceflight; launch is a particularly loud phase [6]. Besides hearing, noise causes irritation, headaches, poorer sleep and relaxation, and worse speech communication and task performance [1]. Crews may become used to loud noise and stop perceiving it as harmful, so measurements matter more than impressions [1].

Example: on Shuttle mission STS-40, Spacelab averaged 68 dBA, up to 84 dBA during ergometer use; crews had trouble communicating, were irritated during work and sleep, and reported headaches [1].

On ISS most noise comes from fans; dust-clogged fans, exercise devices, experiment hardware and noisy activities raise levels, and cleaning the fans brings levels back to normal [1][2]. Acoustic blankets near the treadmill (2016) and the new treadmill (2019) eliminated hazardous exposures while running [2].

## Limits (NASA-STD-3001 and ISS flight rule)

NASA-STD-3001 Volume 2 (acoustics brief, 2023) [1]:
- On orbit, A-weighted sound level must stay **below 85 dBA** regardless of duration (hazardous noise limit); impulse noise **below 140 dB peak** at the head.
- Launch and entry: at most 105 dBA at the ear; launch abort: at most 115 dBA; 24-hour noise dose no higher than the equivalent of 85 dBA for 8 hours.
- Hearing protection cannot be used to meet the design requirements.

ISS Noise Level Constraint Flight Rule (3-dB exchange rate) [2][4]:
- **72 dBA** 16-hour work-day average (LEQ16): above it crew are directed to wear hearing protection during noisy activities.
- **60 dBA**: the flight surgeon may recommend hearing protection depending on the individual and the exposure.
- **85 dBA**: noise hazard level; hearing protection required whenever exceeded.
- Sleep (8-hour LEQ8): **50 dBA** is the sleep disturbance level; **62 dBA** is the limit for adequate hearing rest.

## Measured exposure on ISS

From NASA acoustic dosimetry up to March 2021 [2]:
- Average work-day exposure has stayed below 72 dBA since ISS assembly was completed, with some noisier increments (2012, 2014, 2018). Recent averages were about 69 dBA in both the US and Russian segments.
- The 85 dBA hazard level was exceeded in about **41% of crew-worn measurements since 2008**, without counting hearing protection; about 43% of these events came from crew activities and communications (meals, conferences) rather than equipment.
- Sleep-period averages stayed below 62 dBA but above 50 dBA (about 54-56 dBA); crew surgeons discuss sleep problems with crew at private medical conferences. Sleep stations are adequately quiet when the door is closed.

## Monitoring and hearing checks

- **Acoustic dosimetry**: crewmembers wear an acoustic dosimeter for 24 hours (work day and sleep) and static monitors are placed in noisy areas; this is done about **every other month** (every 60 days) [2][3].
- **Hearing protection** is always available: foam and custom earplugs and active noise-reduction headsets [2]. A Noise Hazard Inventory lists locations and activities where protection is recommended or mandatory [2].
- **Hearing assessments**: NASA performs hearing assessments before, during and after missions [5]. On ISS, crews self-administer On-Orbit Hearing Assessments, and the JSC audiologist may give individual hearing protection advice based on the results [4].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Urgently:
- sudden hearing loss, ringing in the ears, vertigo or nausea after an EVA or a pressure change: these are inner-ear signs of decompression sickness [7] (see the decompression sickness and ear barotrauma documents);
- hearing loss after an impulse noise (bang, pressure release) or with ear pain or discharge.

At the next PMC:
- a change on the on-orbit hearing assessment, persistent ringing, muffled hearing or difficulty understanding speech on the loops;
- poor sleep attributed to noise, or a sleep station that feels loud (report so levels can be measured) [2].

Practical tips from NASA data: keep the sleep station door closed, report noisy fans for cleaning, and wear protection during exercise or other activities listed as noisy [2].
