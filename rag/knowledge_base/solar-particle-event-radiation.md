## What a solar particle event is

Space radiation comes from three sources [1]:
- **Galactic cosmic rays (GCR)**: continuous, from all directions; shielding is not effective (on average about 7% dose reduction) because particles fragment into secondary radiation.
- **Solar particle events (SPE)**: bursts of low to medium energy protons from the Sun, infrequent, most often near solar maximum. Shielding IS effective: aluminum and polyethylene give on average about 50% dose reduction.
- **Trapped particles** (South Atlantic Anomaly on ISS, Van Allen belts on the way to the Moon): a secondary contribution to long-term risk; normal SPE shielding also covers them.

GCR dose and SPE probability are anti-correlated over the 11-year solar cycle. SPEs cannot be accurately predicted in advance, but most past events showed dose-rate rises that real-time monitoring can detect in time to reduce exposure well below NASA limits [1]. About 95% of SPEs require only ALARA ("as low as reasonably achievable") measures and do not approach dose limits [1].

## Recognition

- An alert from the onboard radiation monitoring system, which by requirement must alert crew and operations teams when exposure rates exceed predefined thresholds (V2 6164), or from ground space-weather monitoring [1].
- At NASA, the Space Radiation Analysis Group (SRAG) at JSC supports missions using crew-worn dosimeters and area monitors [2].
- A sudden rise in personal dosimeter dose rate compared with the crewmember's usual daily dose (see the radiation dose reference document) is the key data signal. There are no body symptoms at the start of an event.

## Immediate actions

Follow the flight control team and SRAG instructions. NASA design guidance describes these protective steps [1]:
1. **Stop EVA**: real-time alert dosimetry gives a very high level of protection if the EVA is terminated within 2 hours and crew can reach shielding. EVA is the most exposed activity, especially on the lunar surface.
2. **Go to the most shielded location**. Dose differs with position inside a vehicle: in a NASA capsule example, four crewmembers at different locations would receive 210 to 259 mSv from the same event.
3. **Build the storm shelter**: add about 10 cm water equivalent around the crew quarters by rearranging water containers and high-hydrogen-content stowage; this protects against a once-in-100-years event. A once-in-1000-years event would need about 20 cm. SPE timelines usually allow enough time to reconfigure.
4. **Keep wearing the personal dosimeter** and record readings and shelter times.
5. Stay sheltered until the ground declares the event over.

## In-flight and surface specifics

- Natural shielding: Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field equal about 1000 g/cm2; Mars' thin atmosphere about 42 g/cm2; the Moon has none (0 g/cm2) overhead, although standing on the lunar surface already gives significant protection compared with free space [1].
- For missions longer than 6 months NASA recommends vehicle shielding of at least 20 g/cm2 against SPEs [1].
- NASA's SPE limit: the design reference event (the sum of the October 1989 events) must give less than 250 mSv effective dose (V1 4031) [3]. Career limit: less than 600 mSv (V1 4030) [3][1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Acute radiation syndromes from an SPE can include nausea, vomiting, fatigue, skin injuries and depletion of blood-forming organs [1]. Contact the flight surgeon:
- if nausea, vomiting, unusual fatigue or skin reddening appear during or after an event (do not attribute them automatically to motion sickness or illness);
- if the crewmember could not reach shelter or was on EVA during the event;
- if the dosimeter or ground estimate shows a dose approaching the event limit.

The sources read do not give dose thresholds for each symptom; dose assessment belongs to SRAG and the flight surgeon.

## Follow-up

- SRAG combines dosimeter and area monitor data to determine each crewmember's mission exposure and long-term risk [2].
- Long-term effects of exposure are increased cancer risk and possible changes in cognition, motor function and behavior [1].
- NASA requires personal monitoring of each crewmember's exposure and alerting when rates exceed set thresholds (V2 6162, V2 6164) [1]; keep the event in the crewmember's dose history.
- Record the event dose against the crewmember's career total; NASA manages all exposures with the ALARA principle [1][2].
