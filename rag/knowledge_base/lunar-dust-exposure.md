## Recognition

Apollo crews brought dust into the lunar module on their suits; it was ubiquitous in the cabin, very hard to clean from the hands, and floated everywhere once the vehicle returned to microgravity, getting into eyes, nose and lungs [1]. Reported effects [1]:
- **Eyes**: ocular irritation in each case, treated with copious saline irrigation.
- **Nose**: congestion in most crewmembers, attributed to the 100% oxygen atmosphere, dust and pre-flight viral exposure; a decongestant gave moderate relief. Symptoms eased on the lunar surface but returned in microgravity as dust floated in the cabin.
- **Skin**: the dust is abrasive; under fingernails and inside gloves it adds to EVA hand injuries (see the EVA glove document).

Health hazards listed by NASA [1]: eye irritant, skin abrasion, lung (pulmonary) toxicity, and possible allergic effects (research pending). Apollo 12 crew kept their helmets on before docking because of floating dust [1].

## Why lunar dust is different

- Particles range from about 0.02 to 10 micrometres; **95% are smaller than 2 micrometres** and 40% smaller than 0.1 micrometre. The smallest particles reach deepest into the lungs [1].
- Grains are angular with sharp edges, like volcanic ash, carry an electrostatic charge and may be chemically reactive because of solar wind, radiation and micrometeoroids [1].
- In rat inhalation studies with real Apollo 14 dust, lunar dust was **moderately toxic** compared with reference dusts, causing lung inflammation and septal thickening that increased with dose [1].
- For the eyes, a NASA laboratory study of Apollo 14 dust (rabbits and a human-cell cornea model; one dust type only, not highland or polar dust) found it **minimally irritating**: slight conjunctival redness and swelling at 1 hour that resolved within 24 hours, no corneal scratches on fluorescein staining, and a rating of "not classified" for eye irritation under the UN-GHS system [2]. The authors note that contact lens wearers may be more susceptible and recommend an eyewash station if grains enter the eye [2].
- Polar ice may contain toxic volatiles (hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, mercury); these are a concern for special samples from permanently shadowed regions, handled by containment, not for ordinary regolith [1].

## Immediate actions

1. **Eyes**: irrigate with plenty of water or saline, as Apollo crews needed [1] (see the eye irritation document for the full procedure). Fully shielded goggles can be used if dust is a nuisance [2].
2. **Nose and skin**: rinse nasal passages and clean the body; NASA's Apollo lessons ask for the ability to clean the body and rinse eyes and nasal passages [1].
3. **Cabin**: clean suits before or after entry, use the vacuum cleaner and wipe surfaces; on Apollo the ventilation did not remove dust well, while a vacuum cleaner helped with larger particles [1].
4. **Peaks of exposure** (for example right after EVA ingress) can be reduced with temporary dust masks and goggles [1].
5. Report symptoms at the post-EVA private medical conference.

## In-flight specifics and limits

- **Exposure limit** (NASA-STD-3001 V2 6053): lunar dust particles smaller than 10 micrometres in the cabin air must stay below a time-weighted average of **0.3 mg/m3** during intermittent daily exposure for up to 6 months [1].
- Lung effects build up over weeks to months, so NASA has not set an acute (short-term) limit; average exposure matters most [1].
- The vehicle must monitor celestial dust and alert the crew when levels approach the limits; monitoring is expected mainly after EVAs and during anomalies rather than continuously [1].
- The limit applies to the lunar south pole without extra margin [1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

- Eye pain, blurred vision or light sensitivity that does not settle after irrigation (NASA lists eye abrasion as a lunar dust risk [1]).
- Cough, wheezing, chest tightness or breathlessness after dust exposure, or a falling SpO2 compared with the in-flight baseline.
- Nasal congestion or sneezing that persists or spreads to several crewmembers (also check the cabin dust reading).
- Skin abrasions or nail-bed injuries that look infected.

## Follow-up and data to monitor

- Cabin dust monitor readings after each EVA and the time-weighted average against 0.3 mg/m3 [1].
- Per-crewmember symptom log: eyes, nose, cough, skin.
- SpO2 and breathing rate trend for anyone with respiratory symptoms.
- Use of goggles, dust masks and cleaning after each EVA, and whether eye or nose symptoms followed ingress [1][2].
- Post-mission, dust monitoring data become part of each crewmember's occupational exposure record [1].
