## Recognition

Skin conditions are among the most commonly reported medical events in spaceflight [2]. They rarely threaten life or the mission, but diagnosis and treatment are harder with limited resources [2].

What crews experience:
- **Minor trauma**: a review of the US program found 219 in-flight musculoskeletal injuries; hand injuries were the most frequent, typically abrasions and small lacerations [1]. Exercise is the most frequent source of injury on ISS, and EVA accounted for 0.26 in-flight injuries per EVA [1]. EVA gloves have been cut or punctured on several Shuttle missions, probably by burrs or sharp edges [6] (see the EVA glove and hand injury document).
- **Rashes and dermatitis**: viral reactivation (herpes, shingles), contact dermatitis or eczema-like patches, and skin infections are common skin conditions in flight [2].
- **Burns**: thermal burns are among possible EVA injuries [6]; burns secondary to fire are on NASA's list of conditions to be ready to treat [5]. For fire-related burns and smoke see the fire document.
- **Electrical shock**: possible with damaged insulation or failed equipment isolation [4].

Burn depth (NASA brief) [3]: first degree affects only the top skin layer; second degree involves the top layer and part of the dermis; third degree destroys epidermis and dermis and may reach the deeper fat layer.

## Space-specific facts

- Spaceflight thins the epidermis (reported at about 30 micrometers at the end of a 6-month mission, normal range 27-150; reversible after return) and impairs the skin barrier; some crews report dry, itchy, thinning skin, increased sensitivity or delayed wound healing, and the immune system is dysregulated [2].
- For a 950-day six-person Mars mission, skin and subcutaneous infections are among the most likely infection events, together with respiratory infections [7].
- **Touch-temperature thresholds** (NASA-STD-3001) [3]: skin at 15-43 C is safe for unlimited contact; 43-49 C hot or 0-15 C cold causes pain and performance loss; **49 C or above** causes tissue damage and **0 C or below** risks frostbite. At 49 C skin temperature, a third-degree burn takes about 8 minutes. Numbness of the fingers appears around 7 C skin temperature. Metal surfaces heat or cool the skin much faster than plastic or Velcro [3].
- **Electrical current thresholds** (NASA-STD-3001) [4]: the startle threshold (involuntary muscle contraction) is 2 mA DC or 0.5 mA AC; above 40 mA DC or 8 mA peak AC a person may lose muscle control. Wet skin and large contact areas lower body resistance [4].

## Immediate actions

1. **Make it safe**: stop contact with the hot, cold or energized surface; for an electrical injury, make sure power to the equipment is removed before touching the crewmember (NASA requires the ability to interrupt power and to show whether equipment is energized) [4].
2. **Bleeding**: for anything more than a small cut follow the major trauma document.
3. **Photograph** the wound, burn or rash with a scale reference and send it to the ground; NASA uses skin photodocumentation in flight [8] and remote diagnosis of skin conditions is a recognized challenge [2].
4. **Request a PMC**: wound closure (suturing), dressing choice and any antibiotic, antiviral or steroid cream are decided by the flight surgeon; suturing is listed among in-flight procedures [5].
5. After any electrical shock or a burn caused by contact with equipment, report the hardware so it can be made safe [3][4].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Urgently for:
- any electrical shock with loss of consciousness, palpitations, chest pain or muscle pain or weakness (see the chest pain document);
- any burn that looks deeper than the top skin layer (second or third degree, see burn depth above) [3], a burn on the face, eyes or hands, or any burn from fire or chemicals;
- a deep cut, a cut that keeps bleeding, or a cut with numbness or loss of finger movement;
- a painful band-like rash on one side of the body or near the eye (possible shingles; see the immune document).

At the next PMC: slowly healing small wounds, spreading redness, itching rashes, dry or cracked skin.

## Follow-up and data to monitor

- Daily photo of the wound or rash in the same light and position, to judge healing remotely [2][8].
- Signs of infection: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, fever compared with the crewmember's in-flight temperature baseline (see the infection document).
- Some crewmembers report delayed wound healing in flight [2]; report wounds that are not improving.
- Note which activity caused the injury (exercise device, translation, EVA suit, hardware edge) so the cause can be fixed [1][6].
