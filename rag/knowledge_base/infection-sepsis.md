## How common are infections in flight

- A study of 46 long-duration ISS missions (more than 20 flight-years) reported 12 infection events, all minor: about 0.6 events per person per flight-year [1].
- NASA's 2025 microbiology brief states that, thanks to the pre-flight Health Stabilization Program and in-flight cleanliness, there has not been a case of infectious disease during flight on ISS [3]. The two statements differ in definition: minor infections (such as skin or upper respiratory) do occur, but no serious infectious disease outbreak has been recorded.
- NASA's Integrated Medical Model ranks **sepsis (mainly urosepsis) as the third most likely reason for an emergency evacuation from ISS**, after kidney stones [4].
- Latent herpesviruses are shed by 53% of astronauts on short flights and 61% on long ISS missions [1] (see the immune document).
- Looking ahead: up to about 90 infection events are estimated for a 950-day six-person Mars mission, mostly respiratory and skin or subcutaneous infections; experts rank infections and sepsis first of 30 conditions for mission impact and among the lowest for survivability [1].

## Why risk is higher in space

- **Immune dysregulation**: reduced T-cell function and a pro-inflammatory state during flight [1].
- **Microbes change**: 25 of 29 ISS bacterial isolates (86%) were resistant to at least one antibiotic, versus 44% of isolates from an Antarctic station [1]. Airborne droplets from talking, coughing or sneezing stay suspended in microgravity, so air filtration is essential [3].
- **Drugs**: antibiotics show reduced potency and shorter shelf life in space [1].
- **Before flight**: the Health Stabilization Program (quarantine of crew and close contacts, vaccinations) starts 14 days before launch at NASA/JSC [2]. Before it was introduced, 57% of Apollo crewmembers had some infectious illness before flight; since Apollo 14 it has been used before every flight [2].
- NASA lists in-mission infectious conditions a program may need to treat: pharyngitis, influenza, gastroenteritis, respiratory infection, herpes zoster reactivation, and sepsis [5].

## Recognition

Infection signs: fever or chills, a localized source (burning urination or flank pain; red, warm, swollen skin or pus; cough, sore throat or breathlessness; abdominal pain, vomiting or diarrhea), unusual fatigue.

**Judge fever against the crewmember's own in-flight baseline**: resting core temperature rises by about 1 C over the first 2.5 months of long-duration flight [6], so a terrestrial fever cut-off can give false alarms. A rise above the in-flight baseline together with symptoms is what matters.

Sepsis is a severe infection with low circulating volume, vessel dilation and organ dysfunction [1]. In practice this shows as blood pressure falling and heart rate rising compared with the in-flight baseline, and signs of failing organs such as confusion or drowsiness, breathing difficulty or falling SpO2, and little or no urine. No sepsis score has been validated in microgravity, and hospital early-warning scores use Earth reference ranges; compare with the crewmember's own trend.

## Immediate actions

1. Measure temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate and SpO2, and compare with the in-flight baseline (see the in-flight vital signs reference).
2. Look for the source: urine dipstick for urinary symptoms (see the urinary document), photographs of skin lesions (see the skin document), throat, chest and abdominal symptoms.
3. Request a private medical conference. Choice of antibiotic, antiviral or antifungal and any IV fluids are flight surgeon decisions; NASA lists antibiotics, antiseptics, antifungals, antivirals and supportive care to prevent sepsis as medical treatment capabilities [3].
4. Reduce spread: keep air filtration running and follow cleaning and hygiene procedures; NASA microbial control relies on HEPA filtration, cleaning and personal hygiene [3].
5. If sepsis warning signs appear, ask for an urgent conference: evacuation may be the only definitive care in low Earth orbit.

## Microbiome changes (HRP risk of host-microorganism interactions)

NASA HRP lists the Risk of Host-Microorganism Interactions. Ongoing studies suggest that the community of microbes on astronauts' skin and in their gut changes in space, but how this affects the chance of disease is unknown; some microbes have been found more virulent in spaceflight [7]. Researchers are uncertain whether the risk comes from microbiome changes, from changes in the ability of microbes to cause disease, or from shifts in immune function, and these may amplify each other [7]. Precautions include limiting crew exposure to large groups in the two weeks before launch; researchers are still assessing how disinfectants, anti-inflammatories and antibiotics could help [7]. Crew provide biological samples and swab station surfaces so microbial changes can be monitored [7].

## Limits of care on board

- Point-of-care blood tests exist on ISS, but blood cultures and inflammatory markers are hard to perform; ultrasound is the main imaging tool [1].
- Large volumes of IV fluid, a key part of sepsis treatment, are unlikely to be available because of stowage limits; a device that makes IV fluid from drinking water was tested in flight in 2011 [1].
- Cardiovascular deconditioning can make a septic crewmember's circulation less stable and changes the response to vasopressor drugs [1].
- Invasive organ support (mechanical ventilation, dialysis, vasopressor infusion) would be very difficult and survival would be low [1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Urgently for: any sepsis warning sign above; fever with flank pain or with urinary retention (risk of urosepsis [4]); fever with a rash near the eye; rapidly spreading skin redness; inability to keep fluids down.

At the next PMC: mild cold symptoms, a localized minor skin infection, or a slightly raised temperature without other signs.

## Follow-up and data to monitor

- Temperature, heart rate, blood pressure and respiratory rate as often as the flight surgeon directs, plotted against the in-flight baseline.
- Fluid intake and urine output.
- Antibiotic course: doses taken and side effects; remember reduced potency of older stock [1].
- Symptoms in other crewmembers (possible spread in a closed cabin, where droplets stay airborne [3]).
- New cold sores or a band-like rash, since latent herpesviruses reactivate in many crewmembers during flight [1].
