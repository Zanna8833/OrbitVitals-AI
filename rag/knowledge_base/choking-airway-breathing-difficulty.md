## Choking: recognition

A crewmember who suddenly cannot speak, cough effectively or breathe, often grabbing the throat during or after eating, has a likely airway obstruction. In microgravity, floating food crumbs, liquid droplets and small objects are an added risk because they do not fall away; NASA notes that particles float and are more likely to enter the eyes and airways [3].

## Choking: immediate actions (ISS Checklist 1.105)

From the ISS emergency procedure "1.105 Choking" (version dated 2015, released by NASA under FOIA) [1]:

**If the patient is awake:**
1. Position yourself behind the patient.
2. Make a fist and place it above the belly button but below the rib cage.
3. Pull sharply inward and upward (abdominal thrusts).
4. Repeat until the object clears or the patient becomes unconscious.

The checklist gives no microgravity-specific modification. The microgravity CPR guideline describes its reverse bear hug technique as a modified Heimlich position that works without restraining patient or rescuer [2].

**If the patient becomes unconscious:**
1. Open the airway with head tilt-chin lift.
2. Look for the foreign object and remove it only if easily reachable.
3. Pinch the nose and attempt to give breaths.
4. Go to the AED-assisted CPR procedure (see the cardiac arrest document) [1].

**When the object clears:** take pulse, respiratory rate and blood pressure with the blue Medical Diagnostic Pack, record them, and call MCC-H for a private medical conference with the surgeon [1].

## Difficulty breathing (ISS Checklist 1.108)

For any crewmember with shortness of breath, the checklist procedure is [1]:
1. Count pulse and respiratory rate for 15 seconds each and multiply by four; record.
2. Give oxygen through a low-flow non-rebreather mask from the Respiratory Support Pack (follow the cue card in the lid).
3. Give inhaled albuterol: shake, exhale fully, inhale slowly while activating the inhaler, hold the breath 8-10 seconds; deliver a second dose.
4. Apply the pulse oximeter and record SpO2.
5. Inspect mouth, nose, neck and chest for abnormal anatomy, secretions and increased work of breathing; listen to both lungs front and back.
6. Measure blood pressure and pulse.
7. Collect history: events before symptoms, medications, fever, cough, severity.
8. Call MCC-H for a PMC and follow surgeon direction.

Think about causes specific to the spacecraft: toxic gas or smoke (see fire and toxic atmosphere documents), elevated CO2, falling cabin pressure (see rapid depressurization), allergic reaction (see anaphylaxis), or pneumothorax after barotrauma.

## Assisted breathing and airway equipment

- Indications to assist breathing (from the ISS ALS algorithm): blue/grey lips, face or hands; respiratory rate below 12 or above 30 per minute; snoring or difficulty inhaling. Assist with mouth-to-mouth or the AMBU bag at one breath every 5 seconds and add oxygen [1].
- Basic airway items in the red Emergency Medical Treatment Pack: AMBU bag, oral airways, nasal airways (6 and 7 mm) [1].
- Advanced airway items in the yellow Physician Equipment Pack: laryngoscope, endotracheal tubes, intubating laryngeal mask airway (ILMA), bougie, suction device and a variable oxygen system [1].
- The 2020 microgravity CPR guideline recommends that airway management happen only with the patient restrained and at least two rescuers present; a supraglottic airway is preferred unless a skilled intubator is present, and a manual suction device should be readily available, especially during airway management [2].

## When to contact the flight surgeon and follow-up

Contact immediately for: any choking episode that needed thrusts (risk of injury and aspiration), SpO2 that does not improve with oxygen, respiratory rate below 12 or above 30, stridor, cyanosis, or altered consciousness.

Follow-up: repeat respiratory rate, SpO2 and lung sounds over the next hours; new cough, fever or chest pain after choking may indicate aspiration and needs surgeon review. Compare SpO2 with the crewmember's own baseline (see vital signs reference document).
