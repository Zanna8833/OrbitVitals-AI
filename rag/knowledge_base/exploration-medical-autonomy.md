## Three mission classes

NASA's mission duration brief compares [1]:

| | Low Earth orbit (ISS) | Lunar missions | Mars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Communication | near real-time (1-1.5 s) | minor latencies | extended latencies (0-40 min) |
| Evacuation | available | 3-11 days | none |
| Resupply | strong | limited | none |
| Onboard medical capability | relies on Earth | restricted | restricted; limited ground support, autonomous care |

The CPR guideline for exploration missions estimates the Mars-Earth delay at **3 to 23 minutes each way** [2].

For 60 years NASA has relied almost completely on real-time support from experts on the ground; with up to 40 minutes of delay, crews must solve more problems themselves with the supplies on board, and teamwork with the ground suffers [1].

## Medical consequences

- **Emergencies**: during CPR on a Mars mission telemedicine is impractical and should only be attempted if a crewmember not involved in treatment is free; the decision to stop CPR is made by the most medically qualified crewmember, alone if necessary [2].
- **Care planning**: NASA sizes in-flight medical capability by mission duration, distance from Earth, time to definitive care and required autonomy [3]. Knowledge and tasks can be shared among crewmembers, medical devices, artificial intelligence systems and robots [3].
- **Medications**: missions of 1-5 years exceed usual expiration dates of 1-2 years, with no resupply and more radiation [4].
- **Behavioral health**: for missions beyond 6 months or beyond LEO, NASA recommends extra unobtrusive objective monitoring and more support for crew-ground communication [5]. Psychiatric problems become especially important when evacuation is impossible and real-time talk with specialists is not possible [2].

## What the crew must be able to do alone

With delayed or absent ground support, the crew needs to [1][2][3]:
1. Recognize red-flag conditions quickly from symptoms and onboard data, comparing with each crewmember's own baseline.
2. Start time-critical treatment (CPR, anaphylaxis, bleeding control, atmosphere emergencies) from onboard procedures without waiting for the ground.
3. Send complete, structured reports (vital signs, timeline, photos, medications given) so the ground can reply usefully despite the delay.
4. Manage common conditions (sleep, congestion, pain, minor injuries) with the onboard kit.

## Implications for an onboard assistant

- Retrieve the right onboard procedure fast; do not depend on a live connection to Earth.
- Emphasize individual baselines and trends, since there is no quick lab or specialist.
- Prepare concise reports for the delayed message to the flight surgeon.
- Be explicit about uncertainty, and never replace the crew medical officer's or flight surgeon's judgment.

These implications are design conclusions drawn from the sources above, not NASA requirements.
