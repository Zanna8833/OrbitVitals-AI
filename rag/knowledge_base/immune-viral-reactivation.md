## What happens to immunity in flight

A 2018 review by NASA and international scientists (Crucian et al.) summarizes [1]:
- Dysregulation of the immune system, and reactivation of latent herpesviruses, **persist for the whole duration of a 6-month orbital flight**.
- Some parts of adaptive immunity are reduced, while some parts of innate immunity are heightened.
- Despite isolation from Earth pathogens, there is an increased incidence of infectious disease, increased allergic symptoms and **persistent skin hypersensitivity reactions in some crewmembers**.
- It is not possible to predict which crewmember will be affected.

Causes include psychological and physical stress (stress raises cortisol, which suppresses immunity), radiation, altered nutrition, disrupted sleep and microgravity [1]. NASA's food brief notes that inadequate nutrition in space has been linked to impaired immune function [4].

The review warns that these changes, combined with longer duration and galactic cosmic radiation, may increase clinical risks on deep-space missions, so a broad countermeasure approach is needed [1].

## Latent virus reactivation

Most adults carry herpesviruses that stay dormant (latent) after childhood infection. In flight [1]:
- Reactivation and shedding of these viruses may act as a biomarker of weakened adaptive immunity.
- **Varicella-zoster virus (VZV, the chickenpox/shingles virus)**: 50% of astronauts shed live, infectious VZV in saliva without symptoms during short flights, and 65% during long-duration flights. A few cases turned into clinical shingles (herpes zoster).
- VZV reactivation can cause painful shingles with possible long-lasting nerve pain, and more rarely other complications.

## Countermeasures in use

- **Before flight**: the Health Stabilization Program (quarantine, screening, vaccinations) lowers the risk of carrying infections into space [2]. NASA screens crew, for example for MRSA in the nose and for H. pylori [1].
- **Vehicle**: HEPA air filters, water filters, water biocides and pasteurization, contamination-resistant surfaces, condensation control and trash containment [1].
- **Shingles**: options described include pre-flight shingles vaccination and preventive antiviral treatment such as valacyclovir [1]. The 2015 ISS kit contains valacyclovir 1 g tablets [3].
- **Nutrition and exercise**: adequate nutrition, vitamin D status and exercise are being studied for immune protection; probiotics are a possible future countermeasure [1].

## Recognition: what to report

Report at the next private medical conference, or sooner if severe:
- a band-like painful rash or blisters on one side of the body or face (possible shingles);
- cold sores or mouth ulcers;
- new rashes, itching or allergy-like symptoms that persist;
- fever, sore throat, cough or other infection signs (compare temperature with the crewmember's in-flight baseline, which runs higher than on Earth);
- slow wound healing.

Contact the flight surgeon promptly for a rash near the eye, a rash with fever, or any facial weakness, severe headache or vision change.
