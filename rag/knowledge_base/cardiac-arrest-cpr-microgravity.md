## Key principles

The only published guideline dedicated to CPR in spaceflight is the 2020 guideline of the German Society of Aerospace Medicine and the European Society of Aerospace Medicine Space Medicine Group (Hinkelbein et al., Scand J Trauma Resusc Emerg Med 2020;28:108) [1]. Its main points:

- CPR in microgravity is feasible and follows Earth guidelines (AHA/ERC) for the fundamentals: fast recognition, high-quality chest compressions, compression depth and compression-to-ventilation ratio [1].
- What changes is **how** compressions are delivered (no gravity to push against), airway management and defibrillation [1].
- CPR is split into **Basic Life Support (BLS)** at the site, and **Advanced Life Support (ALS)** once the patient is restrained at the medical station with equipment [1].
- Cardiac arrest in flight is expected to be very rare, and survival after arrest is likely lower than on Earth because intensive post-resuscitation care is not available [1]. Evidence is mostly from parabolic flights and manikins; no real in-flight cardiac arrest has occurred.

## Immediate actions: Basic Life Support at the site

1. Recognize: crewmate unresponsive and not breathing normally. Call out, trigger the medical emergency alarm, and get the other crew [1].
2. Start chest compressions immediately; do not wait for equipment or restraint [1].
3. **Evetts-Russomano (ER) method** first: the rescuer places their left leg over the patient's right shoulder and the right leg around the patient's torso, interlocks ankles behind the patient's back, and compresses the chest without being pushed away [1].
4. If ER does not allow adequate compressions, switch to the **Reverse Bear Hug (RBH)**: rescuer encircles the patient's chest from behind (a modified Heimlich position) and compresses [1].
5. ER is preferred at the site because it does not depend on cabin width and a third crewmember can move rescuer and patient together, with compressions ongoing, to the medical equipment and restraint system [1].
6. Start CPR even if no defibrillator is available [1].

## Advanced Life Support at the medical station

1. Restrain the patient on the **Crew Medical Restraint System (CMRS)**, which has been used since Shuttle mission STS-81 (1997) for fast fixation of patient and provider [1].
2. Switch to the **Handstand (HS) method** if cabin dimensions and rescuer height allow: patient's back on a solid surface, rescuer's feet against the opposite wall, arms extended overhead, hands on the sternum, generating force by flexing and extending hips and knees [1]. HS gave the best manual compressions in studies (about 45-47 mm depth, about 115 compressions/min) and was the least tiring for the rescuer [1]. Even HS did not reach the terrestrial 50-60 mm depth target in studies [1].
3. If HS is impossible: restrained CPR in the standard (side) position or straddling position on the CMRS [1].
4. An automated chest compression device may be used on a restrained patient if one is carried, but installing it must not delay compressions (weak recommendation) [1].
5. Rotate rescuers to limit fatigue.

## Airway, defibrillation and drugs

- **Airway**: only once the patient is restrained and at least two rescuers are present. If no one is well trained in intubation, use a second-generation supraglottic airway; tracheal intubation remains the gold standard for a skilled provider, with patient and rescuer restrained on the CMRS. Have a manual suction device ready [1].
- **Defibrillation**: use the AED only on a patient restrained on an electrically isolated surface such as the CMRS, to protect other crew and vehicle electronics. Defibrillate when indicated (VF/pulseless VT). A defibrillator is part of the ISS crew health care system [1].
- **Vascular access**: only if more than two rescuers are present and compressions remain high quality. Peripheral IV first, tibial intraosseous if IV fails. Use a degassed infusion bag inside a pressure bag; it was used effectively in parabolic-flight trauma studies and is simpler and faster than an electric infusion pump [1].
- Drugs and doses: per ISS medical checklist / flight surgeon; the guideline gives no space-specific drug doses.

## Telemedicine, Mars distance and termination

- In low Earth orbit, consult the flight surgeon when it can be done without reducing CPR quality; the crew must always inform Mission Control of the emergency [1].
- On a Mars mission, one-way delay of about 3-23 minutes makes real-time support useless during the first minutes of CPR; attempt contact only if a crewmember not involved in treatment is free [1].
- The decision to stop resuscitation is made by the crewmember with the highest medical qualification, after consulting telemedicine if possible; if not possible, that crewmember decides alone. Continuing futile CPR also consumes limited drugs and equipment needed for future emergencies [1].
- The guideline gives no recommendations for post-resuscitation care because evidence for intensive care in microgravity is absent [1].

## ISS Medical Checklist algorithm (2015 version)

The ISS emergency medical procedures released by NASA under FOIA (Medical Checklist pages dated 2015) describe the on-board sequence [3]:

1. Assess the environment for hazards, then the patient. If unresponsive and not breathing or only gasping, start CPR [3].
2. With two rescuers: one starts CPR; the other relocates and restrains the patient on the Crew Medical Restraint System, pages another crewmember and calls MCC-Houston for a private medical conference [3].
3. CPR cycles: **30 chest compressions to 2 breaths** (1 second each), 5 cycles (about 2 minutes) [3].
4. Deploy the AED: attach electrodes, power on, follow voice prompts (read the screen if prompts are inaudible). After each "Start CPR" prompt, do a 10-second pulse check [3].
5. Advanced life support: tibial intraosseous access with the IO device; drugs from the ALS medication kit in the red Emergency Medical Treatment Pack (epinephrine 0.1 mg/mL prefilled syringes; atropine if no shock advised, lidocaine if shock advised, per the checklist algorithm) [3].
6. After return of pulse: stop compressions and drugs, assist breathing (one breath every 5 seconds with the AMBU bag if needed), give oxygen, check breath sounds, attach BP/ECG and pulse oximeter, call MCC-H [3]. Assisted breathing is indicated if lips/face are blue-grey, respiratory rate is below 12 or above 30 per minute, or the patient is snoring or struggling to inhale [3].
7. **Termination** (coordinate with the surgeon if available): consider stopping only if all apply: at least 10 minutes of CPR since the last drug dose, no pulse or spontaneous breathing, and at least 45 minutes since resuscitation began [3].

Kit contents and algorithms change between increments; the current on-board checklist always takes precedence.

## After the event

- Record times (collapse, first compression, shocks, ROSC or termination), rhythm strips and drugs given for the flight surgeon.
- Behavioral health support for the crew is essential after a resuscitation attempt on a close crewmate; the guideline highlights unresolved ethical and psychological issues [1].
- Medical capabilities on spacecraft explicitly include physical restraints and evacuation-vehicle accommodation for an ill crewmember [2].
