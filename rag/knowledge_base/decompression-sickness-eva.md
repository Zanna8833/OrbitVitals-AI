## Why EVA causes decompression sickness

The ISS cabin is at 14.7 psia, while the US EVA suit (EMU) operates at 4.3 psia with 100% oxygen [1][4]. When ambient pressure falls quickly, nitrogen dissolved in tissues comes out as bubbles (Henry's law) [1]. Bubbles can be:
- **Venous gas emboli (VGE)**: often asymptomatic at low load; high load can cause cough and shortness of breath [1].
- **Arterial gas emboli (AGE)**: from lung overexpansion or right-to-left shunting of venous bubbles; can cause stroke-like symptoms, loss of consciousness, seizures, cardiac ischemia [1].

NASA has never had a Type II (serious) DCS case in spaceflight [1]. The only anecdotal astronaut DCS symptom was reported years later in an autobiography [2]. NASA warns that mild cases may go unreported: aspirin taken before EVA and normal suit aches can mask pain-only DCS, and symptoms often clear on repressurization [2]. The first reported case could be a serious one [2].

## Recognition: types and timing

NASA classification [1]:
- **Type I**: joint/musculoskeletal pain, itching, mild rashes, single-limb tingling or numbness, skin mottling (cutis marmorata).
- **Type II**: neurological symptoms (numbness, weakness, cognitive problems, confusion, impaired balance), inner ear problems, cardiopulmonary symptoms.

Most cases appear 60-240 minutes after decompression; inflammatory effects can last more than 24 hours [1]. Diagnosis is clinical, based on symptoms: bubbles on ultrasound do not prove DCS and their absence does not exclude it [1].

Risk factors [1]: patent foramen ovale (about 25% of people have one; NASA does not screen it out), dehydration, higher body fat, and strenuous lower-body or resistive exercise after decompression. Exercise before decompression helps wash out nitrogen.

Type II symptoms in more detail (NASA PFO brief, 2026) [5]: neurological (numbness, tingling, weakness, unsteady gait, bladder control problems, paralysis, confusion), inner ear (ringing in the ears, hearing loss, vertigo, nausea, vomiting, poor balance) and cardiopulmonary (dry cough, chest pain behind the breastbone, shortness of breath).

## Patent foramen ovale (PFO) and EVA - 2026 NASA brief

NASA published a medical technical brief on PFO in November 2026 [5]:
- A PFO is an opening between the right and left atrium that stays open in about 25% of adults (about 6% have a large one) [5]. It lets venous gas bubbles bypass the lung filter and reach the arteries and brain, which can turn a bubble load into arterial gas embolism or stroke-like DCS [5].
- Anything that raises right-atrial pressure, such as a Valsalva maneuver or straining, can push blood and bubbles across a PFO [5].
- NASA **does not disqualify** astronaut candidates with a PFO; all candidates are checked for PFO (bubble-contrast echocardiography) at their first annual exam, and treatment choices are made with the flight surgeon considering PFO size, mission length and EVA plans [5].
- Antiplatelet or anticoagulant treatment for a PFO adds bleeding risk, and NSAIDs or aspirin taken in flight for pain add to it; flight surgeons weigh this for EVA [5].
- Ground experience: at NASA's Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory, 79,568 dive hours since 2015 produced 5 DCS events; divers there are not screened for PFO [5].

What this means for the assistant: a crewmember with a known PFO who develops any neurological, inner-ear or chest symptom after EVA or a pressure drop is a Type II DCS suspect until the flight surgeon says otherwise.

## Immediate actions during an EVA

1. Report any joint pain, tingling, numbness, skin change, visual or neurological symptom to the EVA team immediately; do not "wait and see" [2].
2. Actual or suspected DCS usually means terminating the EVA; on ISS the EVA terminate can take up to about 30 minutes [2][3].
3. Breathe 100% oxygen and return to the airlock; returning the crewmember to cabin pressure may itself resolve symptoms [4].

## In-flight treatment on ISS

ISS treatment keeps the crewmember in the suit for the high oxygen concentration [2]:
1. Suit pressure (4.3 psid) stacked on cabin pressure (14.7 psia) gives about 19.0 psia with more than 95% oxygen. NASA's model predicts 91-97.5% probability of symptom resolution [2].
2. If symptoms persist, the Bends Treatment Apparatus on the suit relief valve raises suit pressure to 6.0 or 8.0 psid (total 22.7 psia); predicted resolution 94-98.5%; modeled time from recognition to full bubble resolution about 2 h 55 min [2].
3. A minimum of 16.4 psia may alleviate symptoms, but the protocol depends on the scenario [1].
4. Fluids (oral or IV) are recommended for DCS except pulmonary DCS cases; other drugs per flight surgeon [1].
5. After successful treatment the crewmember is excluded from reduced-pressure activities for 24 hours [3].

The terrestrial standard (US Navy Treatment Table 6 in a hyperbaric chamber) is not available in flight, but sub-terrestrial treatment is expected to be adequate for altitude DCS [3].

## Exploration missions (8.2 psia / 34% O2)

Exploration cabins are planned around 8.2 psia with 34% oxygen (lunar surface) or 10.2 psia (cislunar), which shortens prebreathe [2]. With an exploration suit able to repressurize to 8.2 psid, immediate repressurization stops bubble growth, but predicted symptom resolution is only 48-67%, so DCS would still likely end the EVA; modeled time to bubble resolution about 3 h 49 min [2].

## Prevention: prebreathe

Prebreathing 100% oxygen washes nitrogen out of the tissues before decompression [1]. Validated ISS protocols include the 4-hour in-suit prebreathe, the Campout protocol (sleeping in the airlock at 10.2 psia / 26.5% O2, limited to 8 h 40 min), the exercise prebreathe and the In-Suit Light Exercise (ISLE) protocol [1]. Any mask leak or breath of cabin air is a **break in prebreathe** and requires extra prebreathe time or a restart [1]. NASA-STD-3001 Vol 2 Rev F requires protocols validated to less than 4% DCS probability with 95% confidence and no serious DCS in ground tests [1].

Crew actions: stay well hydrated, report any prebreathe break, avoid strenuous exercise right after decompression.

## When to contact the flight surgeon / follow-up

- Every suspected DCS, even if symptoms resolved on repressurization.
- Immediately for any Type II sign: weakness, numbness, confusion, visual or balance problems, chest pain, shortness of breath.
- Follow-up: neurological check, symptom log for at least 24 hours, hydration, no reduced-pressure activity for 24 hours after treatment [3].
