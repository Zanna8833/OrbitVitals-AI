## Recognition

A psychiatric emergency is a behavioral change that puts the crewmember, the crew or the vehicle at risk. Signs:
- talk of suicide, self-harm, hopelessness or of harming others;
- acute agitation, aggression, or a conflict that turns physical or threatens safety;
- confusion, disorientation or fluctuating attention (delirium);
- hallucinations, bizarre beliefs or sudden personality change (psychosis);
- dangerous behavior near hatches or critical controls.

NASA lists delirium, psychosis, depression, anxiety, trauma- and stress-related disorders, grief and relationship problems among conditions a program may need to manage in flight [1].

Precedents [2]:
- Soyuz T10-Salyut 7 (1984): the crew reported possible hallucinations to mission control.
- Soyuz T14-Salyut 7 (1985): depression may have contributed to an evacuation and early end of the mission.
- Shuttle: a payload specialist became despondent after an experiment failed; the crew worried about dangerous behavior, including opening a hatch, and Shuttle hatches were then fitted with locks.

## Immediate actions

1. **Safety first**: keep the crewmember away from hatches, critical controls and sharp or hazardous items; the commander decides on securing the vehicle. Do not leave the crewmember alone if there is talk of self-harm.
2. **Rule out physical causes** that can mimic a psychiatric illness: check cabin ppO2 and ppCO2 (NASA lists hypoxia and CO2 exposure among contributors), medical conditions such as a recent head injury or illness, sleep loss, and new medications or drug side effects [3]. Measure vital signs and SpO2.
3. **Call the ground at once**: request an immediate private medical or psychological conference. Medical conferences must be held in a private location, with a way to break in for emergencies [4].
4. **Restraint and sedation are medical decisions**: NASA lists physical or pharmaceutical restraint for physiological and psychological use among in-mission capabilities [1]. Use them only as directed by the flight surgeon and the onboard medical checklist (for kit medications see the medical kit and behavioral health documents).
5. **Document** what happened, times, statements and vital signs for the surgeon.

## In-flight specifics

- Isolation, confinement, workload, sleep loss, family separation and lack of privacy all contribute to behavioral conditions; long missions and communication delay raise the risk [3][2].
- Real-time private audio and video with ground medical and psychological support is a required capability [1][4]. For missions with no real-time contact, NASA suggests asynchronous behavioral health treatment protocols on electronic devices and medical devices for neurobehavioral disorders [1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Immediately (inform the commander as well):
- any statement about suicide, self-harm or harming others;
- hallucinations, confusion, disorientation or a sudden personality change;
- aggression, a physical fight, or any action that endangers hatches, controls or life support;
- a crewmember who cannot perform critical tasks.

At the next private conference: persistent low mood, irritability, sleep problems or interpersonal tension that does not endanger safety (see the behavioral health document).

## Follow-up and data to monitor

- Sleep duration, food intake, work performance and cognitive test results compared with the crewmember's own baseline [3].
- Cabin CO2 and O2 trend at the time of the event [3].
- Medications given, doses, times and effects.
- Crew dynamics after the event; the ground behavioral health team plans further support and workload changes [3].
