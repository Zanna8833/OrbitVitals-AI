## Why spaceflight raises stone risk

Kidney stones form when urine is supersaturated with minerals (most often calcium oxalate, also uric acid, struvite, cystine) [1]. Spaceflight factors [1][3]:
- Bone loss releases calcium into urine (hypercalciuria).
- Headward fluid shift and lower water intake; some crew restrict fluids to avoid time-consuming toileting.
- High sodium and animal protein intake; changes in urine pH and solutes.

NASA's Integrated Medical Model ranks kidney stone as the **second most likely reason for emergency medical evacuation from ISS** [1].

Experience to date: one reported in-flight stone episode (a cosmonaut with severe lower abdominal pain that resolved spontaneously); no US segment crewmember has reported a stone in flight; NASA has recorded 43 stone events in 29 crewmembers since 2016 (mostly outside flight) [1]. Astronaut stone incidence is similar to commercial pilots and about half that of the general population, thanks to screening [1].

## Prevention

From the NASA urinary health brief [1]:
1. **Hydration**: NASA provides at least 2.5 L of drinking/food water per crewmember per day [1][4]. Drink regularly; do not avoid fluids to reduce toilet use.
2. **Exercise** to preserve bone and reduce urinary calcium (see bone and muscle document).
3. **Diet**: manage oxalate, calcium, magnesium and salt intake.
4. **Potassium citrate** raises urine pH and lowers calcium oxalate supersaturation; it reduced stones in bed rest and is available on ISS at the flight surgeon's discretion.
5. Thiazides and bisphosphonates can lower stone risk but have side effects.
6. Pre-flight screening: renal ultrasound for mission-assigned astronauts about 6 months before launch and one month after return; urine stone-risk profile pre- and post-flight.

## Recognition

Symptoms of a symptomatic stone: renal colic (severe, cramping flank or lower abdominal pain that comes in waves, may move toward the groin), nausea and vomiting, blood in urine, signs of infection; obstruction can cause hydronephrosis [1]. The ISS kit urine Chemstrip can detect blood in urine [2].

Differential to keep in mind: back pain of space adaptation (first days, no urinary signs), urinary retention, urinary infection, appendicitis or other abdominal causes.

## In-flight management

1. Contact the flight surgeon for a private medical conference.
2. **Ultrasound**: if a stone is suspected, ultrasound and limited lab work can detect it; NASA has tested guided ultrasound software (AMOS) so non-physician astronauts can scan for kidney stones [1].
3. **Pain and nausea**: NSAIDs and opioids for pain, ondansetron for nausea [1]. The 2015 ISS kit contains ibuprofen, ketorolac (intramuscular), hydrocodone-acetaminophen, hydromorphone (intramuscular) and ondansetron [2]. Doses per flight surgeon.
4. **Tamsulosin** relaxes ureter smooth muscle and may help stones pass faster [1]; kit contains tamsulosin 0.4 mg [2].
5. Hydration with oral or IV fluids per surgeon.
6. Catheter or nephrostomy drainage may be needed if urine flow is blocked [1].
7. Experimental: ultrasound propulsion and burst-wave lithotripsy to move or break stones are under study [1].

Stone size matters for passage: stones around 3 mm pass on their own more than 90% of the time, on average in about 7 days (range 0-40 days) [1].

## Red flags and escalation

Urgent flight surgeon contact / possible evacuation discussion for:
- Fever or chills with flank pain (infected obstructed kidney, risk of sepsis).
- Pain not controlled with available analgesics, persistent vomiting.
- No urine output or inability to void.
- Signs of shock (heart rate rising and BP falling versus in-flight baseline, confusion).

On a Mars mission a stone becomes a mission-threatening problem because evacuation is impossible; prevention and on-board ultrasound are the main tools [1].

## Follow-up

- Fluid intake and urine output log; urine dipstick for blood; pain score; temperature trend vs in-flight baseline.
- Body mass and exercise compliance, because bone loss drives urinary calcium [1][3].
- Symptoms of urinary retention or infection during stone passage (see urinary retention and UTI document).
- After any stone, NASA restricts flight duties (2 weeks after spontaneous passage, 4 weeks after lithotripsy or ureteroscopy) and requires annual 24-hour urine studies [1].
