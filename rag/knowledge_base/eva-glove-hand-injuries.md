## Recognition

Pressurized gloves restrict hand movement and create pressure points; repetitive gripping causes hand fatigue and discomfort [3][1]. Typical findings [1]:
- hand and finger fatigue, abrasions, redness (erythema);
- pain at the fingertips, the knuckles (metacarpophalangeal joints) and between the fingers;
- tingling or numbness (paresthesia);
- fingernail injuries: subungual hematoma (blood under the nail), torn nail and **onycholysis** (fingernail delamination, the nail lifting off the nail bed), most often of the middle finger.

NASA data 1981-2010 (Shuttle and ISS, 4000 Series and Phase VI gloves) [1]:
- 96 crewmembers performed 322 EVAs; at least one glove-related injury was reported on 96 of these EVAs (29.8%), and 50 of the 96 crewmembers (52.1%) reported at least one.
- The 106 in-flight incidents involved 126 injuries; the most common types were fatigue (soreness or cramping), abrasion and paresthesia, mostly at the hand, the knuckle (metacarpophalangeal joint) and the fingers.
- In flight, fingernail problems were recorded as nail trauma (6 cases), subungual hematoma (3) and onycholysis (1); the authors think these may be related to each other.
- In ground training (90 incidents, mostly in the Neutral Buoyancy Laboratory), about 76% of injuries were pain, redness or onycholysis, mainly at the fingernail, the knuckle and the finger crotch (the web between two fingers). The middle fingernail was the most affected for onycholysis.
- Injury recording changed over time and was not standardized, so the numbers are not exact incidence rates [1].

Across the US program, EVA accounted for 0.26 in-flight musculoskeletal injuries per EVA; the most common sites of injury in EVA suits are the hands, feet and shoulders [2].

## Risk factors and prevention

Risk factors for onycholysis in NASA's exploratory analysis (odds ratios; significance threshold p up to 0.15, wide confidence intervals) [1]: female sex (2.6), older age (1.07 per year), longer duration of the event (1.57 per hour), a mismatch between middle finger length and glove finger length (7.7 per inch) and use of the Phase VI glove (8.5). Smaller hand anthropometry, longer suited exposures and poor glove-hand fit were the main risk factors for glove injuries overall; for injuries during EVA flight only hand size was significant (smaller hands, higher risk) [1]. The Phase VI result is uncertain because the authors list several confounders, such as changes in injury reporting over time [1]. Larger finger take-ups to fill a too-long glove finger may contribute to injury [1].

Countermeasures made available to crews after 2002-2004 [1]:
- keep fingernails trimmed short and pay attention to nail hygiene;
- nail hardener or polish;
- bandages over the fingernails.

Other controls: glove sizing checked against hand measurements, tools and procedures that reduce repetitive gripping and overexertion, and shorter suited exposures [1][3]. Glove cuts and punctures from burrs or sharp edges have ended EVAs early (STS-118) [4].

Moon and Mars: lunar dust is abrasive to the skin and gets under fingernails and into gloves [5] (see the lunar dust document).

## Immediate actions after an EVA

1. **Inspect both hands** during the post-EVA medical check (pre- and post-EVA examinations are part of NASA medical operations) [6]: look for redness, abrasions, blood under the nails and nails lifting.
2. **Photograph** any nail or skin injury and report it to the flight surgeon at the post-EVA private medical conference.
3. **Protect an injured nail**: cover it with a bandage, the same countermeasure crews use before EVAs [1]; dressing and any treatment are decided by the flight surgeon (see the skin, wounds and burns document).
4. **Rest the hand**; report pain or weakness that limits grip.
5. **Tingling or numbness** that appears after the EVA, involves more than the pressure points, or comes with other symptoms must be reported at once: numbness and tingling are also neurological signs of decompression sickness [7].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Immediately:
- numbness, tingling or weakness after the EVA that is spreading, involves a whole limb, or comes with joint pain, skin mottling, visual, balance or thinking problems (possible decompression sickness) [7];
- a deep cut through a damaged glove, or any suit breach (also a suit pressure event).

At the post-EVA PMC or soon after:
- nail lifting or blood under a nail, painful fingertips, persistent paresthesia of a finger, abrasions;
- signs of infection of a nail bed (redness, swelling, pus);
- before the next EVA, if any hand injury has not healed, so glove fit and protection can be reviewed.

## Follow-up and data to monitor

- Per-EVA log of hand symptoms, glove size used, suited duration and tasks [1].
- Photo series of injured nails until healed.
- Grip strength and hand fatigue reports across successive EVAs; the analysis found that more experienced crewmembers reported onycholysis less often [1].
- Recurrent injuries with the same glove are a reason to review sizing with the EVA and suit teams [1].
