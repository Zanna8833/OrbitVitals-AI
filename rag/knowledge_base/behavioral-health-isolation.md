## Why behavioral health matters in flight

Spaceflight is an isolated, confined and extreme environment. Common concerns are **stress, depression, anxiety, relationship problems and grief**; they may resolve with time or support, or develop into a psychiatric disorder [1]. NASA notes that reported behavioral health mishaps are few, but the real incidence may be underestimated because astronauts are reluctant to report them [2].

Historical examples [1][2]:
- 2 of 7 (29%) NASA Shuttle-Mir astronauts reported depressive symptoms.
- Soyuz T14-Salyut 7 (1985): depression may have contributed to evacuation and early end of the mission.
- A Shuttle payload specialist became despondent after an experiment failed; crew worried about dangerous behavior including opening a hatch, and Shuttle hatches were fitted with locks.
- Delayed notification of a family death led a crewmember to acute withdrawal and depression.
- Gemini 11: an EVA was ended early because of astronaut fatigue.

## Recognition

Signs the crew or assistant may notice [1][2]:
- Fatigue from sleep loss: irritability, poorer concentration, slower reaction time, errors.
- Low mood, withdrawal from the crew, loss of interest, conflict or tension between crewmembers.
- Anxiety, sleep problems, changes in eating.
- Unusual perceptions (a Salyut 7 crew reported possible hallucinations).

Contributing factors NASA lists: sleep, workload (both overload and long periods of low workload), drug side effects, medical conditions, **hypoxia, carbon dioxide exposure**, food and nutrition, lack of privacy, family separation, limited contact with home [1][2].

Monitoring data: NASA uses a monthly cognitive test (WinSCAT) compared with each astronaut's own pre-flight baseline, with extra testing after an injury or illness [1]. A drop versus baseline, a run of short sleeps (see sleep reference) or falling food intake are reasons to check in.

## Immediate actions

1. **Exclude physical causes first**: check cabin ppCO2 and oxygen (see CO2 headache and cabin atmosphere documents), recent sleep, new medications (many kit drugs list agitation or mood changes as side effects [3]), fever or illness.
2. **Talk privately**: request a Private Psychological Conference (PPC) or Private Medical Conference (PMC); these are scheduled routinely and held in a private location [1][4].
3. **Reduce load**: discuss workload and schedule with the ground; protect sleep (see insomnia document).
4. **Use support resources**: family conferences, crew care packages, personal communication, journaling and Earth photography are used as outlets [1].
5. **Medication only via the flight surgeon**. The 2015 ISS kit lists a "Behavioral Health" section including sertraline 50 mg tablets, lorazepam 1 mg tablets, aripiprazole 5 mg tablets and intramuscular aripiprazole, plus intramuscular diphenhydramine [3]. Choice and dose: flight surgeon.

## When to contact the flight surgeon urgently

Notify the commander and flight surgeon **immediately** if a crewmember:
- talks about harming themselves or others, or behaves dangerously (e.g. near hatches or critical controls);
- shows confusion, hallucinations or sudden personality change (also consider hypoxia, CO2, head injury, drug effects);
- cannot perform critical tasks, or fatigue causes safety errors.

Report at the next private conference: persistent low mood or anxiety for more than a few days, conflict affecting work, ongoing insomnia.

## In-flight specifics and follow-up

- Long missions beyond low Earth orbit raise the risk because of distance, communication delay and duration; NASA recommends additional unobtrusive objective monitoring for missions beyond 6 months or beyond LEO [1].
- Training before flight covers stress, cultural differences, teamwork, small-group living and sleep/circadian strategies [1].
- Repatriation briefings are held pre-flight and normally about 6 weeks before landing with astronauts and families [1].
