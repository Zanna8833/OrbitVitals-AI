## Q: I have waves of flank pain and blood in my urine. Is it a kidney stone? How do I treat it?

Waves of flank or lower abdominal pain with blood in the urine are typical of a kidney stone. Spaceflight raises stone risk through bone calcium loss and low fluid intake [1].

What to do:
1. Contact the flight surgeon for a private medical conference.
2. An ultrasound can look for the stone; NASA has tested guided ultrasound software for non-physician crew [1].
3. Treatment NASA describes: pain medication (NSAIDs, opioids), ondansetron for nausea, and tamsulosin to help the stone pass [1]. The ISS kit contains these; doses are set by the flight surgeon [2].
4. Keep drinking.

Stones around 3 mm pass on their own more than 90% of the time, in about 7 days on average [1].

Call urgently for fever or chills with the pain, inability to urinate, uncontrolled pain or vomiting. See the kidney stones document.

## Q: I can't urinate. What should I do?

Urinary retention happens in flight: NASA has documented 9 in-flight cases (16 with suggestive symptoms), and the odds are 4.5 times higher for female astronauts [1]. Causes include the altered sense of bladder fullness, reluctance to use the toilet early in flight, and motion sickness medications with anticholinergic effects such as promethazine [1].

What to do:
1. Give yourself privacy and time; do not restrict fluids.
2. Tell the flight surgeon which medications you have taken.
3. Contact the flight surgeon. The options NASA lists are a urinary catheter and tamsulosin [1]; the ISS kit contains several catheter types, lidocaine jelly and tamsulosin [2].

Do not wait long: a full bladder is painful, and prolonged retention can lead to infection [1]. See the urinary retention and UTI document.

## Q: How much water should I drink, and what else prevents kidney stones?

NASA provides at least 2.5 L of drinking and food water per crewmember per day, plus 240 mL per EVA hour [3]. Use your allocation and spread drinking through the day. Avoid restricting fluids to reduce toilet trips, a known stone risk factor [1].

Other prevention measures from the NASA urinary health brief [1]:
- Keep up your resistance and aerobic exercise to limit bone loss, which releases calcium into the urine.
- Moderate salt and animal protein intake; manage oxalate.
- Potassium citrate is available on ISS at the flight surgeon's discretion.

Signs of being short of water include low urine output, headache, sleepiness and poor concentration [3]. See the nutrition and hydration document.

## Q: How hard should I exercise, and can I skip ARED this week?

On ISS, cycle protocols are prescribed at 70-100% of pre-flight VO2peak and treadmill protocols at 70-100% of maximum heart rate. A typical aerobic session lasts about 26 minutes [4]. NASA requires crew to keep aerobic capacity and muscle strength at no less than 80% of pre-flight values, and to keep VO2max at or above 32.9 mL/kg/min for missions with microgravity EVAs [4].

Do not skip ARED without agreement from the ground. Resistance exercise is the main protection against bone and muscle loss, and missed sessions add up (see the bone and muscle document). Valid reasons to pause, after discussion with the flight surgeon, include acute pain or injury. If you have been diagnosed with a jugular vein clot, NASA's algorithm stops ARED for at least 30 days [5]. Report any pain during exercise.

## Q: How much should I eat? I'm not hungry and I've lost weight.

NASA requires 100% of your calculated energy and nutrient needs, based on your age, sex, body mass, height and activity, with extra for EVAs and extra vitamin D [6]. In practice, astronauts have historically eaten less than that estimate, about 74% on Shuttle and 79-84% on ISS. Weight loss has occurred even on short missions [6].

What to do:
- Eat regular meals even if not hungry, and choose foods you like.
- Track your body mass against your pre-flight value.
- Report continued weight loss, which also threatens bone and muscle, at your private medical conference.

Poor appetite in the first days is part of space motion sickness [7]. Persistent poor appetite, especially with low mood, nausea or pain, should be checked. See the nutrition and hydration document.

## Q: I've had lower back pain since arriving in space. Is that normal?

Yes, it is common. In a review of 728 astronauts, 52% had space adaptation back pain, mostly mild, starting in the first days as the spine lengthens [8]. Treatments reported as effective [8]:
- curling into the fetal position (91% effective);
- analgesics (85%);
- exercise on the treadmill or cycle (85%).

See the flight surgeon if the pain is moderate or severe and not improving, lasts beyond the first week, follows an injury, or comes with leg or arm numbness, weakness, bladder problems or fever. Flank pain in waves with blood in the urine suggests a kidney stone instead. See the back pain document.
