## Body mass

- On ISS, body mass is measured with special devices because scales do not work in microgravity: NASA's SLAMMD (force = mass x acceleration) and the Russian BMMD (spring oscillation) [1].
- In 25 crewmembers, in-flight body mass averaged **-2.8% (SLAMMD) to -4.4% (BMMD)** versus pre-flight; most loss occurred in the **first 30 days**, after which mass stayed constant for the rest of the mission [1].
- Dietary intake averaged about 80% of the WHO-estimated requirement, and mass loss followed energy intake closely [1].
- The two devices differed by about 1.1 kg on average, so compare readings from the same device [1].

Red flag: continued loss after the first month, or loss greater than the crewmember's early-flight decline, suggests inadequate intake, illness or behavioral health issues; report to the flight surgeon and nutrition team.

## Energy and water intake targets

- NASA-STD-3001: each crewmember gets 100% of calculated energy and nutrient needs (V1 4020) [11]; the food system default is an average of **3,035 kcal/day**, plus **200 kcal per EVA hour** [7].
- Historical ISS intake: about 2,300-2,450 kcal/day; vitamin D is supplemented above Earth DRIs because of no sunlight [7].
- Water: NASA requires at least **2.5 L per crewmember per day** for drinking and food rehydration, plus **0.24 L per EVA hour**; actual ISS intake averaged about 2.0-2.3 L/day [8].

## Bone mineral density

- Without effective countermeasures, weight-bearing sites (hip and spine) lose about **1-1.5% of areal BMD per month** (Mir cosmonauts, 4-14 month flights); this monthly loss exceeds yearly losses in elderly people [2].
- With the Advanced Resistive Exercise Device (ARED, up to 600 lbf), adequate energy intake and normal vitamin D, BMD was maintained in most regions over 4-6 month missions [4]. ARED reduced bone loss versus the older device, but post-flight decreases in femoral neck and hip remained; adding weekly oral alendronate (70 mg) attenuated nearly all bone loss indices [5].
- **NASA-STD-3001 requirement**: countermeasures must keep hip and spine BMD at or above **95% of pre-mission** values and femoral neck at or above **90%** (V1 4027) [11][3].
- BMD (DXA/QCT) is measured before and after flight, not in flight [2][3]. Hip trabecular bone may not fully recover even when DXA BMD recovers [2]. In flight, bone status can only be inferred indirectly (exercise compliance, urine calcium, nutrition).

## Muscle and strength

- In 9 ISS crewmembers after 6 months (with treadmill, cycle and the older resistance device), **calf muscle volume fell 13%** (soleus -15%, gastrocnemius -10%), **peak power fell 32%** and force-velocity characteristics fell 20-29% [6].
- The authors concluded that the exercise prescription and hardware needed improvement; ARED was introduced later [6][4].
- Muscle loss cannot be weighed directly in flight; trends in exercise loads, strength tests and lean mass (post-flight) are used.

## Aerobic capacity, strength and exercise

- **NASA-STD-3001 thresholds**: in-mission aerobic capacity at or above **80% of pre-mission** (V1 4003) [11]; in-mission skeletal muscle strength at or above **80% of baseline** (V1 4024) [11][9].
- ISS training prescriptions: about 30 minutes of interval or steady-state aerobic exercise and 30-60 minutes of load-bearing resistive training [9].
- Typical ISS sessions (about 180-day missions): treadmill (T2) about 27 minutes at average HR 131-135 bpm with about 68% body-weight loading; cycle ergometer (CEVIS) about 26 minutes at average 137 W; prescriptions at 70-100% of pre-flight VO2peak or HRmax [9].
- Crew who exercised at least 3 times per week, above 70% of age-predicted maximum HR and more than 20 minutes per session, had smaller aerobic decrements on landing day [9].

## EVA fitness thresholds (aerobic capacity and strength)

Aerobic capacity for EVA (NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 Rev C) [9]:
- In-mission VO2max at or above **32.9 mL/kg/min** for missions with microgravity EVAs (V1 4001) [11].
- In-mission VO2max at or above **36.5 mL/kg/min** for missions with Moon or Mars surface EVAs (V1 4002) [11].
NASA notes these are not absolute cut-offs: a crewmember below them may need more time and resources for mission tasks, and activities should be planned around measured fitness [9].

Pre-mission strength (V1 4023, table published in a NASA technical bulletin dated July 2026) [11][10]:
- Minimum: deadlift 1.0 x body weight, bench press 0.7 x body weight.
- Microgravity EVA: deadlift 1.3 x, bench press 0.8 x body weight.
- Extraterrestrial surface EVA: deadlift 1.6 x, bench press 1.0 x body weight.
- Unaided terrestrial egress: deadlift 1.3 x, bench press 0.7 x body weight.
The thresholds come from simulated tasks (capsule hatch egress, rescue of an incapacitated crewmember, walking with supplies, hill climb); capsule egress was used because it is one of the most demanding critical tasks [10]. On ISS, strength is tracked with isometric mid-thigh pull and isometric bench press instead of free lifts [10]. The bulletin notes that many crew did not meet the earlier pre-flight requirement yet completed their tasks, which prompted this reanalysis [10].

Use: a crewmember heading for a surface EVA whose in-flight strength or aerobic tests trend toward these values is a reason to discuss the exercise prescription with the ground strength and conditioning specialists before the EVA.

## How to use these numbers

Compare each crewmember with their own pre-flight baseline and early-flight trajectory. Expected pattern: small early mass drop then plateau; steady exercise loads. Concerning pattern: progressive mass loss, falling exercise loads or skipped sessions over weeks, which predict bone, muscle and fitness losses that only become measurable after landing. Bone density cannot be measured in flight (DXA/QCT are done before and after flight) [3]; in flight, use body mass, exercise logs and food intake as proxies.
