## Q: I feel nauseous and vomited twice on flight day 2. What do I do, and can I take promethazine?

This is typical space motion sickness. It affects most crew in the first 2-3 days and usually resolves as the brain adapts [1]. What to do:
- Limit head movements, keep a consistent "up" orientation, rest and keep your head still.
- Sip fluids to avoid dehydration.

Promethazine is NASA's treatment of choice and can be given by mouth, intramuscularly, intravenously or as a suppository; intramuscular or rectal use is preferred if you are vomiting. In Shuttle data, intramuscular promethazine relieved symptoms in most treated crew, 90% within 1-2 hours [1]. The dose is set by the flight surgeon.

ISS crew have daily private medical conferences for the first five days, so report your symptoms there [1]. Call earlier if vomiting is persistent, you cannot keep fluids down, or you have severe headache, confusion or abdominal pain. See the space motion sickness document.

## Q: One side of my neck is swollen and painful, and my face looks puffier than usual. Could it be a jugular clot?

It could, so treat it seriously. Jugular vein blood clots have been found on ISS, mostly through routine ultrasound rather than symptoms. Signs NASA lists are neck vein swelling, facial or eyelid swelling beyond normal space puffiness, headache and SANS-like symptoms [2].

What to do:
- Request a private medical conference soon. An ultrasound of the neck veins can be done by crew with real-time guidance from the ground [2].
- Do not start any blood thinner without the flight surgeon. If a clot is confirmed, NASA's algorithm uses an anticoagulant and restricts heavy resistance exercise and EVA [2].

Call urgently for chest pain, sudden breathlessness, coughing blood, severe headache, or new weakness or speech problems. See the venous thrombosis document.

## Q: I have chest pain and an irregular heartbeat. What now?

Treat this as urgent:
1. Stop activity and rest.
2. If the person becomes unresponsive and has no normal breathing, start CPR (see the cardiac arrest document).
3. Request an urgent private medical conference.
4. Measure heart rate, blood pressure and SpO2, and record times and symptoms.

The 2015 ISS kit contains nitroglycerin 0.4 mg and aspirin 325 mg tablets, plus advanced life support drugs and an AED [3]. Use them only as directed by the flight surgeon or the onboard advanced life support procedure. Other causes to consider include a blood clot (see the venous thrombosis document), anxiety, high CO2 and stimulant medication.

Atrial fibrillation occurs in about 5% of active astronauts, similar to the general population but at a younger age; after 6 months on ISS the left atrium was transiently larger, but no episode of atrial fibrillation was recorded in the 13 astronauts studied [6]. If the pain started after an EVA or a pressure change, think of decompression sickness. No public NASA chest pain checklist was found; follow the flight surgeon. See the chest pain and arrhythmia document.

## Q: I've had a cold sore and a rash for a week. Is this virus reactivation?

It might be. Latent herpesviruses reactivate in many astronauts. For example, live varicella-zoster virus (the chickenpox/shingles virus) has been found in the saliva of 50% of astronauts on short flights and 65% on long flights, a few of whom developed shingles [4]. Some crewmembers also have persistent skin hypersensitivity reactions [4].

What to do:
- Photograph the rash and report it at a private medical conference.
- The ISS kit contains the antiviral valacyclovir [3]; its use is a flight surgeon decision.

Contact the flight surgeon promptly for a painful band-like rash on one side of the body, a rash near the eye, fever, or vision changes. See the immune document.

## Q: A solar particle event alert has been issued. What do we do?

Follow the flight control team's instructions. NASA guidance [5]:
1. Stop any EVA. Real-time alerting is highly protective if the EVA ends within 2 hours and you can reach shielding.
2. Move to the most shielded area. Dose depends on where you are inside the vehicle.
3. Build the storm shelter by moving water containers and hydrogen-rich stowage around the crew quarters (about 10 cm of water equivalent for a once-in-a-century event). SPE timelines usually allow time to do this.
4. Keep your dosimeter on and log shelter times.

About 95% of SPEs need only standard precautions and do not approach the limits [5]. Report nausea, vomiting, unusual fatigue or skin reddening after an event to the flight surgeon. See the solar particle event document.
