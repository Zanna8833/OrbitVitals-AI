## Recognition

Signs of heat stress: feeling hot, heavy sweating, rising heart rate for the same workload, fatigue, headache, dizziness, nausea, slowed thinking or clumsiness; in a suit, helmet fogging and sweat pooling.

Why it matters in spaceflight:
- **Exercise in microgravity**: during exercise on ISS, maximal core temperature was on average 1.39 C higher than on Earth and exceeded 40 C in some astronauts; resting core temperature also rises by about 1 C over the first 2.5 months of flight [2]. A crewmember starts exercise from a higher baseline than on Earth.
- **Pressure suits**: body temperature rises faster in pressure suits than in shirt-sleeve conditions, and heat can quickly reach tolerance limits [1].
- NASA marks the start of **performance impairment** when skin temperature rises by more than 1.4 C, core temperature by more than 0.6 C, or **pulse exceeds 140 beats per minute** [1].
- EVA mishaps: on the first spacewalk (Voskhod 2) the workload exceeded the suit cooling capacity and the suit filled with perspiration to the knees; heating and cooling failures have caused helmet fogging; on ISS-36 water collected in a suit and helmet, impaired vision and risked suffocation, and the EVA was terminated [3].

## Immediate actions

During EVA:
1. Report feeling hot, sweating, fogging or any water in the helmet to the EVA team at once; **water in the helmet is an emergency** and has ended EVAs early [3].
2. Slow down or pause the task: the suit standard aims to keep heat storage below the performance-impairment line so complex tasks can be done safely [1].
3. Use the suit's thermal control and follow the EVA team's instructions; physiological data (heart rate, ECG, body temperature) and metabolic rate displays support these decisions [1].
4. Drink from the in-suit water supply: NASA requires at least **240 mL of water per hour** of EVA [1].

During exercise in the cabin: no specific NASA heat procedure was found in the public sources reviewed. Because exercise core temperature peaks are higher in flight than on Earth [2], report dizziness, nausea, confusion or unusual breathlessness during exercise, and log heart rate for the workload so it can be compared with the crewmember's usual values.

## In-flight specifics

- Suited heat-storage limit (NASA-STD-3001 V2 11011): stored body heat during nominal suited operations must stay between +3.0 and -1.9 kJ/kg, calculated with thermal models [1].
- Suited humidity: 25% to under 75% is the nominal range indefinitely; drier air is allowed only for limited times [1].
- Metabolic rates during EVA (kJ/h): microgravity ISS and Shuttle EVAs average 950 (minimum 575, maximum 2,320); Apollo lunar surface EVAs averaged 1,030 with peaks of 2,607; a simulated 10-km lunar walk-back averaged 2,505 [1]. Surface EVAs will demand more energy than microgravity EVAs [1].
- ISS EVAs typically last 6-8 hours plus 2-3 hours in the suit for preparation and airlock operations [1]. The food system adds **200 kcal per EVA hour** [4].
- On long missions where ground support is delayed, automated suit algorithms may become the main way to monitor physiology [1].
- Hot surfaces: skin contact above 43 C causes pain and above 49 C tissue damage [5] (see the skin, wounds and burns document).

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Immediately:
- confusion, fainting, vomiting, or a heart rate that stays high after stopping exercise or EVA work;
- water in the helmet, or any EVA terminated for thermal reasons [3];
- a crewmember who cannot drink or keeps feeling hot after cool-down.

At the post-EVA or next private conference: heavy fatigue after EVA, recurrent overheating during exercise, low fluid intake on EVA days.

## Follow-up and data to monitor

- EVA heart rate, metabolic rate and suit temperature data against the impairment signs above [1].
- Core temperature trend, remembering the higher in-flight baseline and exercise peaks [2].
- Fluid intake and body mass on EVA and heavy exercise days [1][4].
- Note suit thermal performance problems, helmet fogging and humidity complaints for the suit team [1][3].
