## Sources of toxic exposure on a spacecraft

In a closed cabin the crew breathes the same recirculated air for the whole mission, so any release matters [1]. NASA lists these sources [2]:
- System fluids: ammonia (external thermal control loops of the US segment and Orion), ethylene glycol, Freon 218.
- Payload chemicals (e.g. fixatives such as formaldehyde), batteries (electrolytes), corrosion products.
- Off-gassing of materials (formaldehyde and others), thermal degradation and fires.
- External contaminants such as propellant reaction products; hardware failures.

Exposure can be by inhalation, ingestion, or skin/eye contact [2]. Historical events: Apollo-Soyuz crew inhaled nitrogen tetroxide during descent and developed delayed lung fluid (treated with steroids, hospital for 6 days); STS-40 refrigerator motor overheating released formaldehyde; STS-55 waste bags produced methyl sulfide odors [2].

ISS hazard levels: 0 nonhazardous, 1 least hazardous, 2-3 hazardous, 4 most hazardous; containment rule of thumb is "hazard level + 1" layers [1].

## General spill or release: immediate actions

Most releases cannot be detected automatically (a leaking water bag, broken food packet, failed toilet pretreat bottle) [1]:

1. Warn other crew and keep them away from the affected area [1].
2. Manually annunciate the toxic atmosphere emergency if needed: intermodule fans stop so the contaminant does not spread, and key systems are protected [1].
3. Crew nearby don the right personal protective equipment for the substance (mask, goggles, gloves) and contain it [1].
4. If the release cannot be contained or worsens, isolate the module and plan with Mission Control [1].
5. Check the material's hazard information (safety data sheets must be accessible on board) [2].
6. Crew routinely wear PPE (mask, glasses, gloves) when first opening a hatch after dynamic operations as a precaution [2].

## Ammonia leak: memorized ISS response

Ammonia is the most dangerous toxic scenario on ISS: high-pressure external cooling loops could leak through the interface heat exchanger into the cabin, and there is no way to remove large amounts of ammonia from the cabin air [1].

- **Assume any unexpected toxic atmosphere alarm is ammonia** unless a crewmate has just reported a specific spill [1].
- Memorized response [1]:
  1. Don protective masks.
  2. Evacuate from the US segment to the Russian segment or the US commercial crew vehicle.
  3. Close hatches to isolate the US segment.
- Crew who went to the Russian segment on portable breathing apparatus switch to respirators with ammonia cartridges, purging the hood (breathe in through the filter, exhale into the hood, slide hands over the head to push air out) to avoid eye and lung injury [1].
- If the Russian segment is contaminated, crew go to their Soyuz and use its filter system, or dilute low levels with fans [1].
- Commercial crew vehicle crews don PBAs, retreat to their vehicle, establish clean air and prepare for return to Earth [1].

## Symptoms by contaminant

From the NASA toxicology brief [2]:
- **Ammonia**: low concentrations cause cough and nose/throat irritation; high concentrations cause immediate burning of the nose, throat and airways, possibly respiratory distress or failure. Pungent smell.
- **Formaldehyde**: eye and respiratory irritation; carcinogenic with prolonged exposure.
- **Carbon monoxide** (mostly from fire): headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain, confusion.
- **Freons/halocarbons**: low toxicity; main risk is heart rhythm disturbances, generally only above 1% concentration.
- **Noxious sulfur compounds** (stored waste): nausea, headache.
- **Particles/dust**: float in microgravity and irritate eyes and airways.

## SMAC limits for key contaminants

NASA Spacecraft Maximum Allowable Concentrations, JSC 20584 Rev C (2024), in ppm [3]:

| Contaminant | 1 h | 24 h | 7 d | 30 d | 180 d | 1000 d |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ammonia | 30 | 20 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Carbon monoxide | 425 | 100 | 55 | 15 | 15 | 15 |
| Formaldehyde | 0.8 | 0.5 | 0.1 | 0.1 | 0.1 | 0.1 |
| Ethylene glycol | 25 | 25 | 5 | 5 | 5 | not set |
| Benzene | 40 | 6.7 | 0.5 | 0.1 | 0.07 | 0.013 |

1-hour and 24-hour SMACs are emergency limits (reversible effects allowed, no impairment of judgment); 7-day to 1000-day SMACs protect against any adverse effect during continuous exposure [2]. For mixtures, NASA toxicologists sum hazard indices of compounds with similar effects; air is acceptable if the combined index is below 1 [2]. CO2 is no longer covered by SMACs (removed in Rev C) and is managed separately [3].

## Medical care and escalation

1. Remove the crewmember from the contaminated volume to clean air; keep breathing protection until air quality is confirmed.
2. Eye or skin contact: decontaminate per the ISS medical checklist and the substance's safety data sheet.
3. Contact the flight surgeon for any crewmember with cough, breathing difficulty, chest tightness, eye pain, or exposure above emergency SMACs.
4. Monitor breathing, SpO2 and symptoms for hours afterwards: lung injury from irritant gases can be delayed, as in Apollo-Soyuz [2].
5. Record substance, estimated concentration, duration and PPE used for the toxicologist.
