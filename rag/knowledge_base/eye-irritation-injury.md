## Why eyes are at risk in space

NASA lists ophthalmic conditions that programs may need to treat in flight: eye penetration by a foreign body, chemical burn, corneal ulcer or infection, retinal detachment, acute glaucoma, and SANS-related vision changes [2]. Particles do not settle in microgravity, and lunar dust is a specific exploration hazard: NASA limits lunar dust under 10 micrometers in habitat air to a time-weighted average of 0.3 mg/m3 during intermittent daily exposure (V2 6053) [5].

Example: on STS-100 an astronaut had severe eye irritation during a spacewalk from the anti-fog solution used on the helmet visor [4].

For vision changes without pain or irritation, see the SANS document.

## Recognition

- Foreign body or irritation: gritty feeling, pain, tearing, redness, light sensitivity, blinking.
- Chemical exposure: burning pain after contact with a liquid, gas or dust; may affect several crewmembers (consider a toxic atmosphere event).
- Lunar dust (Moon missions): on Apollo, dust floated through the cabin after returning to microgravity and got into crewmembers' eyes; in each case the eye irritation needed copious saline irrigation [6]. A NASA laboratory study of real Apollo 14 lunar dust (three rabbits and a human-cell cornea model, one dust type only) found it minimally irritating: slight conjunctival redness and swelling at 1 hour that resolved within 24 hours, and no corneal scratches on fluorescein staining. The authors recommend no special precautions, but fully shielded goggles if the dust is a nuisance, an eyewash station if irritating grains enter the eye, and note that contact lens wearers may be more susceptible [7]. In the Apollo records the crews' response was to put on their helmets until the cabin filters cleared the dust, and no injury was reported [7]. See the lunar dust document.
- The ISS checklist includes a general eye exam procedure, and the kit has an eye chart for vision checks [1].
- Warning signs: reduced vision, a visible object stuck in the eye, irregular pupil, severe pain, injury from a high-speed object.

## Immediate actions

From the ISS toxic exposure procedure (2015) [1]:
1. **Rescuer safety**: if a chemical release is the cause, take respiratory, eye and skin precautions before helping, to limit the number of crew exposed.
2. **Remove and discard contact lenses**.
3. **Flush immediately with water from a drink bag**, then set up the eyewash station (on ISS, in the Service Module) and irrigate until the pain resolves or the surgeon says to stop.
4. **Call the ground** and request a private medical conference; if communication is unavailable, perform the general eye exam.
5. **History**: what substance, how long, which eye.
6. **Photograph** the affected eye and downlink for the surgeon.

NASA requires at least **500 mL of potable water per crewmember for eye irrigation** [3].

## ISS kit eye items (2015)

The kit contains [1]:
- tetracaine 0.5% anesthetic eye drops (to allow examination and irrigation);
- fluorescein strips (stain to show corneal scratches);
- antibiotic eye drops and ointment (moxifloxacin, tobramycin with dexamethasone, erythromycin);
- olopatadine 0.2% allergy drops;
- lubricating drops and ointment (carboxymethylcellulose, mineral oil/petrolatum);
- an eye chart.

Use of anesthetic, stain and antibiotic drops is directed by the flight surgeon. Anesthetic drops relieve pain only briefly and do not treat the cause.

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Contact immediately for:
- any chemical exposure to the eye (after starting irrigation);
- decreased vision, a penetrating injury, or an object that cannot be rinsed out (do not press on the eye);
- severe pain, or pain not improving after irrigation;
- redness with discharge or worsening pain over a day (possible infection or corneal ulcer);
- flashes, floaters or a curtain over part of the vision (possible retinal detachment).

## Follow-up

- Recheck vision with the eye chart and compare with the crewmember's baseline.
- Keep photos in folders labeled with crew name and GMT day [1].
- Report particulate or dust sources so the cause can be fixed (filters, cleaning materials).
- Record which eye, the substance, time of exposure, irrigation duration and any drops used.
- A slow change in near vision without pain or redness is more likely SANS; see the SANS document.
