## The five hazards of human spaceflight

NASA's Human Research Program (HRP) groups the challenges of spaceflight into five hazards [1]:
- **Space radiation**: ionizing radiation outside Earth's magnetic field, considered one of the most hazardous aspects of spaceflight.
- **Isolation and confinement**: behavioral responses of groups far from Earth, isolated and confined in a small space for a long time.
- **Distance from Earth**: instructions, new supplies and medical care become harder to receive as astronauts go deeper into space.
- **Gravity fields**: muscles, bones, inner ear and organs must adjust to microgravity, to the Moon, Mars and Earth on return.
- **Hostile/closed environments**: temperature, pressure, lighting, noise, microbes and immune responses inside the vehicle.

NASA states that the hazards do not stand alone: they can feed off one another and worsen effects on the body [1]. On Mars, crews would live and work in about three-eighths of Earth's gravity for up to two years [1].

## How the 17 risks are organized

HRP lists specific risks tied to the five hazards, in three groups: human physiology (11), behavioral performance and human factors (5), and space radiation (1). NASA notes that the effects vary depending on whether the destination is low Earth orbit, the Moon or Mars [2]. The risk names below follow the HRP page as read on 14 November 2026 (page updated 21 July 2026).

## Physiology risks and where this knowledge base covers them

- **Altered Immune System Responses**: stress can reawaken inactive viruses, causing rashes and other symptoms [2]. Document: immune-viral-reactivation.
- **Altered Sensorimotor and Vestibular Function**: motion sickness, hand-eye and gait coordination problems, dizziness during gravity changes [2]. Documents: space-motion-sickness, faq-landing-eva-exploration.
- **Cardiovascular Adaptations**: weightlessness alters circulation and heart and vessel function [2]. Documents: cardiovascular-orthostatic-intolerance, chest-pain-arrhythmia, venous-thrombosis-jugular.
- **Decompression Sickness**: dissolved nitrogen bubbling in tissues when going from the spacecraft to a lower-pressure spacesuit [2]. Document: decompression-sickness-eva.
- **Host-Microorganism Interactions**: skin and gut microbiomes change in space, possibly raising the chance of getting sick [2]. Document: infection-sepsis.
- **Hypoxia**: mild hypoxia is a side effect of the steps used to avoid "the bends" during spacewalks [2]. Document: rapid-depressurization-hypoxia.
- **Inadequate Food and Nutrition**: a balanced diet is essential to crew health and performance [2]. Document: nutrition-hydration.
- **Injury and Compromised Performance from Spacewalks (EVA)**: cognitive fatigue, injury, decompression sickness, low oxygen and high carbon dioxide in the suit [2]. Documents: eva-glove-hand-injuries, heat-stress-exercise-eva, decompression-sickness-eva.
- **Reduced Cardiorespiratory and Musculoskeletal Fitness**: exercise counters losses of bone density, muscle strength and heart and vessel performance [2]. Documents: bone-muscle-loss-countermeasures, reference-body-mass-bone-muscle.
- **Spaceflight Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome (SANS)**: the structure of the eyes and brain can change with spaceflight [2]. Document: sans-vision-changes.
- **Spaceflight-Induced Bone Changes**: without gravity bones break down and become more brittle [2]. Document: bone-muscle-loss-countermeasures.

## Behavioral and human factors risks, and the radiation risk

- **Behavioral Changes and Psychiatric Disorders** (the link text of the page reads "Behavioral Conditions and Psychiatric Disorders"): isolation, radiation, distance and other stressors may affect crew mental health [2]. Documents: behavioral-health-isolation, psychiatric-emergency.
- **Inadequate Teamwork**: communication delays, isolation, limited resources and a much smaller living space than the ISS on a Mars mission make conflict resolution important; crews practice team-building before flight [3]. Document: behavioral-health-isolation (conflict section). No dedicated document.
- **Inadequate Sleep and Irregular Schedules**: chronic sleep loss, constant work demands and irregular biological clocks [2]. Documents: insomnia-circadian-fatigue, reference-sleep.
- **Inadequate Systems Operations Support**: crews must handle unexpected problems with software, vehicles or other systems [2]. Not covered here: it is an operations and training topic, not a medical one.
- **Injury from Dynamic Loads**: crews risk injury during landing activities [2]. Documents: dynamic-loads-landing-injury, faq-landing-eva-exploration, back-pain-spine.
- **Radiation-Induced Cancers**: crews exposed to space radiation may be more likely to develop cancer [2]. Documents: reference-radiation-dose, solar-particle-event-radiation.

## What this list does and does not tell the crew

This is NASA's research-priority list, not a ranking of what is most likely to happen on a given mission and not a set of treatment instructions. For a symptom or an emergency, use the specific protocol document, not this overview. Studies listed by HRP as ongoing are research, not onboard practice.
