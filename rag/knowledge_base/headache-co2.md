## Why headaches are common in space

- **Fluid shift**: in microgravity blood, lymph and cerebrospinal fluid shift toward the head, which may raise intracranial pressure and trigger tension-type or migraine-like headaches. Up to 90% of astronauts report headache in the first few days; most adapt, but headaches can recur throughout the mission [3].
- **Space adaptation**: headache comes with nasal congestion, facial fullness, nausea and vomiting in the first week [3].
- **Carbon dioxide**: CO2 accumulates in a closed cabin. On ISS, each 1 mmHg increase in CO2 doubled the odds of a crewmember reporting a headache; age, time in flight and CO2 level were all associated with headache [2]. 19 of 49 astronauts in Expeditions 2-31 reported headaches; most were moderate and many needed analgesics [1].
- **Poor sleep, dehydration, eye strain** can contribute. NASA notes that fluid shift produces symptoms similar to CO2 over-exposure, and that local CO2 pockets not seen by fixed sensors may explain apparent CO2 sensitivity [1].

## Recognition: what CO2 headache looks like

NASA anecdotal associations between cabin ppCO2 and symptoms [1]:
- Headaches at 2.8-4.5 mmHg, worsening with higher CO2, with fatigue, malaise and flushing.
- Chronic cough, poor sleep, blurred vision and **frontal headache** at about 3.5 mmHg.
- Fatigue, malaise, decreased sleep and nausea above 4.5 mmHg; facial (5th cranial nerve) dysesthesia at 4.5-5.0 mmHg.

Clues pointing to CO2: several crewmembers in the same module report headache at the same time; headache appears in poorly ventilated spots (sleep stations, behind panels, during exercise) and improves after moving to well-ventilated areas.

## Immediate actions

1. Check cabin ppCO2: current value, 1-hour and 24-hour averages, and the module where the crewmember works and sleeps. The NASA limit is 3 mmHg 1-hour average [1].
2. Improve local ventilation: make sure the sleep station and work area fans are running and nothing blocks airflow (CO2 pockets form without ventilation) [1].
3. Move to a well-ventilated module for a while and see if the headache improves.
4. Drink fluids, rest, and avoid screen-heavy work if possible.
5. Analgesics: NASA lists sleep, exercise, hydration, coffee and analgesics (acetaminophen, aspirin or NSAIDs) for space headache [3]. The 2015 ISS kit contains acetaminophen 325 mg, aspirin 325 mg and ibuprofen 400 mg tablets with daily maximums (acetaminophen no more than 12 tablets/24 h, ibuprofen no more than 8 tablets/24 h, take with food) [5]. Follow the flight surgeon's direction for dose and choice.
6. Log the headache (time, severity, location, module, ppCO2) so the ground can correlate it with CO2 data.

## When CO2 is high: crew and ground actions

- If the average ppCO2 stays above limits for days, the Flight Control Team identifies and repairs degraded scrubbers (CDRA) or activates supplemental CO2 removal [1].
- Keeping the 7-day average below 2.5 mmHg would keep headache risk below 1% [2].
- CO2 is also listed among possible risk factors for SANS; persistent headache with vision change needs the SANS evaluation [4].

## Red flags: not an ordinary space headache

Contact the flight surgeon promptly for:
- Sudden, severe ("worst ever") headache, or headache with vomiting, confusion, weakness, speech or balance problems, seizure.
- Headache with new vision loss, double vision, or a blind spot (see SANS document).
- Headache with fever and stiff neck, or after a head injury.
- Headache with symptoms of hypoxia, smoke or toxic exposure, or after a pressure change.
- Headache persisting for days despite normal CO2 and analgesics.

## Follow-up and monitoring

- Trend headache reports against 24-hour and 7-day ppCO2 averages per module. The NASA standard is a 1-hour average of no more than 3 mmHg [1].
- Log each headache with time, location (module, sleep station, exercise device), severity 0-10 and other crewmembers affected.
- Track sleep duration and analgesic use; rising analgesic use is a signal to report at the weekly PMC.
- Note that resting core temperature and other in-flight baselines change over months (see vital signs reference).
