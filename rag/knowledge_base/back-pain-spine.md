## Space adaptation back pain

- Back pain in the first days of flight is common: a review of medical records from Mercury to ISS found an incidence of **52% (382 of 728 astronauts)** [1].
- It is mostly mild (86%); 11% moderate and 3% severe [1].
- It typically appears in the first 2-5 days of flight [3].
- Cause: without gravity the normal spinal curves straighten and the spine lengthens, which may cause pain and reduce spinal stability [3]. Returning crew have spines about 6% longer, which can affect suit fit [4].
- It is usually self-limited [1].

## Immediate actions (self-care)

Most effective treatments in the retrospective study [1]:
1. **Fetal positioning** (knees drawn up to the chest, curled posture): effective in 91% of cases.
2. **Analgesic medication**: 85% effective. The 2015 ISS kit includes acetaminophen 325 mg, ibuprofen 400 mg and aspirin 325 mg tablets, with daily limits printed in the kit list (acetaminophen no more than 12 tablets and ibuprofen no more than 8 tablets per 24 hours) [5]; dose per flight surgeon.
3. **Exercise**, mainly treadmill and cycle ergometer: 85% effective [1].

NASA notes treatment of space adaptation back pain with positioning exercises, analgesics and exercise gives pain relief in at least 85% of cases [3].

## When it is not ordinary space back pain

Contact the flight surgeon promptly if back or neck pain comes with:
- numbness, tingling or weakness in the arms or legs, or pain shooting down a limb;
- loss of bladder or bowel control, or new urinary retention;
- fever, or pain after an injury, a fall during landing or an impact;
- flank pain that comes in waves, blood in urine (consider a kidney stone, see kidney stones document);
- moderate or severe pain that does not improve with positioning, analgesics and exercise, or lasts beyond the first week.

The study authors warn that moderate or severe pain not responding to available treatments risks functional impairment and mission impact [1].

## Disc herniation after flight

- U.S. astronauts had a **4.3 times higher incidence** of herniated intervertebral disc than matched controls, with relatively more cervical (neck) herniations, and evidence that the risk is increased **immediately after spaceflight** [2].
- Practical consequence: follow the supervised post-flight rehabilitation program for return to loading, and report new radiating arm or leg pain early [2][4].
- Post-flight musculoskeletal injury risk is also higher because of muscle weakness, reduced aerobic capacity and lower bone density [4].

## Follow-up and monitoring

- Log pain intensity (e.g. 0-10), location and duration daily in the first week, and analgesic use.
- Watch for pain that limits exercise sessions: reduced exercise accelerates bone and muscle loss (see the bone and muscle documents).
- Report persistent pain at the weekly private medical conference.
- Before landing, remember that crew return with spines about 6% longer [4] and that disc herniation risk is 4.3 times higher than in controls, especially soon after flight [2]; plan the return to heavy loading with the rehabilitation team and report any new radiating arm or leg pain.
