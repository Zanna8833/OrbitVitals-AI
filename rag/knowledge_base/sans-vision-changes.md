## What SANS is

Spaceflight-Associated Neuro-ocular Syndrome (SANS) is a group of eye and brain findings seen during and after long-duration spaceflight [1]:

- **Optic disc edema (ODE)**: swelling of the optic nerve head, one or both eyes. Considered the finding with the highest risk to vision.
- **Posterior globe flattening**: the back of the eye flattens where the optic nerve enters, shortening the eye (about 25% of long-duration crew).
- **Chorioretinal folds**: folds in the retina/choroid (choroidal folds in about 25% of long-duration crew). Folds near the fovea can distort or reduce vision.
- **Hyperopic (far-sighted) refractive shift**: near vision becomes blurry. Post-mission shifts of +0.5 to +2.0 diopters have been reported.

The cause is not certain. The leading hypothesis is the headward fluid shift in microgravity, with venous congestion and possibly mildly elevated intracranial pressure; ICP has never been measured in flight [1]. NASA reports that about 70% of long-duration crew show one or more signs of SANS and about 18% are diagnosed with clinically concerning SANS. To date no crewmember has had vision that could not be corrected after flight; all post-flight vision has been correctable to 20/20 or better with updated glasses [1].

## Recognition and signs

Symptoms the crewmember may notice:
- Blurry **near** vision (reading checklists, screens, labels) developing over weeks to months. This is the typical hyperopic shift.
- Needing a stronger pair of "space anticipation glasses".
- Distortion or wavy lines on the Amsler grid, or a blind spot (scotoma) in the visual field.

NASA reports that 29% of short-duration and 60% of long-duration crew have reported vision degradation; risk increases with time in space [1].

Risk factors listed by NASA [1]: longer missions, repeat flyers, a crowded/small optic nerve head, nutritional/genetic factors in the B-vitamin (folate, B12) one-carbon pathway, CO2 pockets, and impaired sleep.

In its usual form SANS is **not an emergency**: it develops slowly over weeks to months. Sudden vision loss, eye pain, double vision, or a severe headache with vomiting are not typical SANS and need urgent flight surgeon contact.

## Immediate actions when a crewmember reports vision change

1. Record what changed: near vs distance, one or both eyes, onset date, constant or intermittent.
2. Test visual acuity (near and distance) and perform an Amsler grid test for each eye separately; compare with the crewmember's own pre-flight and earlier in-flight results [1].
3. Switch to the next stronger pair of prescription glasses. All crew fly with at least 3 pairs of different power for this purpose [1].
4. Note recent symptoms that may matter: headache, sleep quality, cabin ppCO2 in the days before.
5. Report to the flight surgeon at the next private medical conference, or sooner if any red flag below is present. Further imaging (OCT, fundoscopy, ultrasound) is done with ground remote guidance.

## In-flight monitoring schedule (ISS long-duration)

NASA's in-flight SANS monitoring [1]:
- **"Eye weeks"** of 2-3 days at about flight day 30, flight day 90 and 30 days before return (R-30), with additional testing whenever clinically indicated.
- In-flight tests: ocular questionnaire, visual acuity, Amsler grid, fundoscopy, 2-D ultrasound, high-resolution optical coherence tomography (OCT), threshold visual fields.
- A ground remote guider, a subject-matter expert and a biomedical engineer support each session in real time.

Key OCT number: a change in peripapillary total retinal thickness (TRT) greater than about 19.4 micrometers from pre-flight exceeds normal measurement variability and is the earliest sign of optic disc edema [1]. An average increase of 55 micrometers or more, or Frisen grade 1 or higher disc edema, is the threshold NASA uses to indicate a post-flight lumbar puncture [1].

Pre-flight baseline (18-21 months before launch) includes MRI of brain and orbits, refraction, fundoscopy, OCT, tonometry and biometry, so every in-flight value can be compared with that crewmember's own baseline [1].

## Countermeasures and treatment

- **Glasses**: stronger prescription lenses compensate the hyperopic shift [1].
- **CO2 control**: CO2 is listed as a possible risk factor, and NASA links headaches and blurred vision with ppCO2 around 3.5 mmHg; keeping cabin ppCO2 low is a practical measure [1][2].
- **B vitamins**: not an established countermeasure. A NASA HRP flight study, B-Complex, is testing whether a daily B vitamin supplement, taken for six months before flight, during the mission and for 30 days after, can prevent or mitigate swelling at the back of the eye; it also looks at how the individual's genetics may influence the effect [3]. Do not read it as a recommendation to supplement.
- **Lower body negative pressure and thigh cuffs**: under investigation to reduce headward fluid shift; not a proven treatment [1]. In the HRP Thigh Cuff study, crew wear tight thigh cuffs for six hours in each of two sessions and record vision and blood flow changes, with ultrasound of leg and neck veins, to see whether keeping more fluid in the legs helps [4].
- **Medication**: no medication is proven to prevent or treat SANS. Acetazolamide is the only candidate available on ISS, and its side effects (nausea, fatigue, paresthesia, others) limit use; any use is a flight surgeon decision [1].
- Evidence is still limited and partly contested; treatment is case by case [1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Contact promptly (same day) if:
- New loss of acuity not corrected by the next pair of glasses.
- New Amsler grid distortion, a scotoma, or visual field loss.
- Vision change in only one eye, or sudden change.
- Persistent headache together with vision change, or headache with vomiting.
- Eye pain, redness with vision loss, double vision, or transient episodes of vision going grey.

Routine (next PMC): gradual near-vision blur fully corrected by the next pair of glasses.

## Follow-up and long-term notes

- Track acuity, Amsler, OCT TRT change and glasses used over time; trends matter more than single readings.
- Post-flight testing at R+1 to R+3 days includes MRI with an intracranial-pressure protocol [1].
- Globe flattening has persisted in some crew more than 7 years after flight [1], so long-term follow-up continues after the mission.
- Repeat flyers and longer missions carry higher risk, which is relevant for Mars-class missions [1].

## Ongoing HRP studies on SANS (research, not onboard practice)

NASA lists these as ongoing low Earth orbit studies. The pages describe designs only, with no results, and none changes what a crewmember should do today:
- **B-Complex**: daily B vitamin supplement before, during and after flight, with genetic analysis, blood draws and eye exams [3].
- **Thigh Cuff**: veno-constrictive thigh cuffs against the headward fluid shift [4].
- **Brain Fluid Pressure** (Direct ICP Monitoring and SANS Biomarker Identification): measures pressure in the brain, eyes and neck veins shortly after return and compares it with preflight baselines, to test the hypothesis that fluid redistribution raises intracranial pressure; spinal fluid, blood and urine samples may identify biomarkers [5].
- **Spinal Fluid Biomarkers**: looks for biomarkers in spinal fluid taken shortly after landing and months later, compared with head, eye and blood pressure measurements and eye and brain structural changes [6].
