## What it is and how common it is

Space Adaptation Sickness (SAS) is the set of symptoms that appear while the body adjusts to microgravity. Space Motion Sickness (SMS) is the nausea/vomiting part of it. According to the NASA-STD-3001 Medical Technical Brief on SAS [1]:

- SMS affects about 73% of crewmembers in the first days of their first flight; NASA quotes 70-90% for the broader SAS symptom set.
- Symptoms usually start in the first 2-3 days (most crew within the first 3-7 days) and usually resolve in 2-3 days, longer in some crew.
- It is generally worse on a first flight. Having had SAS before predicts recurrence. Age, sex and career vs non-career status do not predict it, and terrestrial car sickness does not reliably predict it either [1].

Cause: a sensory conflict between vestibular (otolith and semicircular canal), visual, proprioceptive and touch inputs once gravity cues disappear, plus the headward fluid shift [1][2]. This is expected adaptation, not a disease, but it can cause dehydration, weakness and reduced performance.

## Recognition and symptoms

SAS (fluid shift and adaptation) symptoms [1]:
- Facial and head fullness, nasal congestion (most evident in the first 3 days).
- Headache (reported by up to 90% of astronauts in the first few days).
- Back pain (space adaptation back pain, about 52% of flyers, days 2-5).
- Drowsiness, constipation, urinary retention.

SMS symptoms [1]: sensitivity to head/body movement, stomach awareness, nausea and vomiting, loss of appetite, pallor, sweating, dizziness, malaise, loss of initiative, irritability, headache, drowsiness.

Things that make it worse: head movements (pitch movements reported as most provocative, then roll and yaw), unpleasant sights, noxious odors, some foods, excessive warmth, losing the "1 g" visual orientation. Things that help: concentrating on a task, firm touch/tactile input, closing the eyes, restricting activity and head motion [1].

## Severity grading (NASA SMS criteria)

NASA uses a 0-3 grading (Barratt et al., cited in the NASA SAS brief [1]):

- **0 None**: nothing except mild transient headache or mildly reduced appetite.
- **1 Mild**: one or a few mild symptoms, may come only with head movements, no operational impact; may include a single episode of retching or vomiting; resolves in 36-48 hours.
- **2 Moderate**: several persistent, waxing/waning symptoms; loss of appetite, malaise, lethargy, epigastric discomfort; no more than 2 vomiting episodes; minimal operational impact; resolves within 72 hours.
- **3 Severe**: pronounced malaise and/or lethargy, strong desire not to move the head, more than 2 vomiting episodes, clear performance decrement; may last beyond 72 hours.

Log the grade at each private medical conference so the trend is visible.

## Immediate actions (crew self-care and crewmates)

1. Reduce provocation: limit head movements (especially pitch), stay restrained in one place, keep a consistent "up" visual orientation, close the eyes if needed [1].
2. Improve air movement around the crewmember and keep them away from odors and heat [1].
3. Keep drinking small amounts often; dehydration is the main health risk. Track fluid intake and vomiting episodes [1].
4. Have emesis bags within reach; secure the crewmember near a ventilation flow.
5. Medication only per flight surgeon / ISS medical kit. NASA's treatment of choice is promethazine (oral, intramuscular, intravenous or suppository; IM or rectal preferred if vomiting prevents oral dosing). Historical Shuttle data: IM promethazine gave symptom relief in most treated crew, 90% within 1-2 hours [1]. Meclizine, ondansetron and other antiemetics have also been flown. Doses are set by the flight surgeon.
6. Prophylactic pre-launch medication did not significantly reduce symptoms in NASA data [1].

## In-flight specifics

- Crew schedules include adaptation time on flight days 1-4, and NASA schedules no EVA within 72 hours of launch so SMS can resolve [1].
- On ISS, private medical conferences (PMCs) are held daily for the first five days after docking (15 minutes per crewmember), then weekly [1].
- Diagnostic tools needed: auscultation device and blood analysis (check electrolytes if vomiting persists) [1].
- Vomiting while suited is dangerous: NASA notes that donning a suit increases the chance of an ill crewmember vomiting inside it, which is one reason EVAs are not scheduled right after a gravity transition [2]. Inside the cabin, keep emesis bags ready because vomitus does not fall away in microgravity.
- Promethazine is sedating on Earth, but sedation was not commonly seen in the first days of flight; stimulants have sometimes been paired with it per flight surgeon [1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Contact the flight surgeon (do not wait for the next PMC) if:
- More than 2 vomiting episodes, or vomiting that prevents keeping fluids or medication down (grade 3).
- Symptoms persisting beyond 72 hours, or appearing for the first time after the first week (consider another cause).
- Signs of dehydration: very low urine output, dizziness, dry mouth, rising heart rate compared with the crewmember's own in-flight values.
- Severe headache, confusion, fever, abdominal pain localized to one spot, or neurological signs (these are not typical SMS).
- An EVA, docking or other critical task is scheduled and the crewmember is not fit.

If SMS is severe, NASA lists IV hydration as an option [1].

## Return to Earth: readaptation motion sickness

On landing, crew may get Terrestrial Readaptation Motion Sickness (TRMS): dizziness, nausea, unstable gait, worsened by head movements, rotation and heat. It combines with reduced blood pressure control and dehydration, raising fainting risk [1]. Most TRMS clears in 2-3 days, and crew are monitored for 30-45 days after landing [1]. See the orthostatic intolerance document for landing precautions.

## Follow-up and monitoring

- Daily SMS grade (0-3), vomiting count, fluid intake, urine output.
- Body mass trend and appetite over the first week.
- Medication taken and response.
- Readiness for scheduled tasks (EVA, robotics). On ISS no EVA is scheduled before 72 hours of flight [2].
- Use the daily private medical conferences of the first five days to review symptoms and medication [1].
