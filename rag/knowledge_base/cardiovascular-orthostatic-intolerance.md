## Cardiovascular changes in flight

- Within about 2 days of reaching microgravity, fluid shifts headward and circulating (intravascular) volume falls by about 15% [1].
- In long-duration ISS flight, stroke volume rose 35% and cardiac output 41%, 24-hour blood pressure fell by about 8-10 mmHg through vasodilation, and heart rate was unchanged [3]. Another study found resting HR, BP and baroreflex unchanged in flight with the ISS countermeasure program [4].
- Bed rest models show cardiac atrophy within 2 weeks and reduced baroreflex sensitivity [1].
- Returning crew have, relative to Earth normal: 12-15% less blood volume, 10-12% fewer red blood cells, 15-20% aerobic deficit, weaker postural muscles, lower bone density, and spines about 6% longer [2].

These changes are expected adaptation in microgravity. They become a problem when gravity returns.

## Orthostatic intolerance: recognition

Orthostatic intolerance (OI) is the inability to maintain blood pressure and brain perfusion when upright after microgravity; it leads to presyncope and syncope [1]. Signs on standing or under acceleration: lightheadedness, tunnel or greying vision, nausea, sweating, pallor, palpitations, weakness, fainting.

How common: about 20% after short-duration flights; in a study of 6 astronauts after 129-190 day flights, **5 of 6 became presyncopal** on post-flight tilt testing [5]. Women are more prone to OI symptoms in tilt tests and in the field [1]. Post-flight, seated resting HR is slightly higher and baroreflex slope lower than before flight [4].

OI is combined at landing with readaptation motion sickness, unstable gait, fatigue and higher risk of musculoskeletal injury [2][6].

## Pre-landing countermeasures

Per NASA briefs [1][2]:
1. **Fluid and salt loading** before deorbit: water with salt tablets or an equivalent solution, amount based on body weight; current protocols use 24-48 oz (about 0.7-1.4 L) of NaCl-water solution. NASA requires at least 1 L of potable water per crewmember for re-entry fluid loading [7].
2. **Orthostatic intolerance garments**: graduated lower-body compression (e.g. about 55 mmHg at the lower leg decreasing to about 20 mmHg at the abdomen) to prevent blood pooling.
3. **Active cooling** to limit peripheral vasodilation and heat stress.
4. **Recumbent seating** to protect brain blood flow during deceleration.
5. **In-flight exercise** (aerobic and resistive) throughout the mission.
6. Medications such as midodrine have limited data and are not in routine use [1].

Daily private medical conferences are held in the 5 days before entry and on landing morning [6].

## Immediate actions if a crewmember feels faint after a gravity transition

1. Get the crewmember lying down (or reclined) immediately; do not let them stand or walk unassisted. Recumbent position protects brain blood flow, the same principle as recumbent seating [2].
2. Keep the orthostatic intolerance garment on; its compression prevents blood pooling in the legs [2].
3. Give oral fluids if alert and not nauseated; IV saline is available at the landing site (minimum 3 L per crewmember) [2].
4. Cool the crewmember if hot [1][2].
5. Check pulse, blood pressure and SpO2; the landing medical kit includes BP cuff, pulse oximeter, thermometer and handheld ultrasound [2].
6. Treat nausea with antiemetics carried by the flight surgeon (promethazine, ondansetron, meclizine) [2].

On Moon or Mars there will be no landing-site medical team: crew must plan to sit or lie for the first period after landing and rely on garments, fluids and recumbent seats.

## When to escalate and follow-up

Escalate (flight surgeon / landing team) for: loss of consciousness, fainting that recurs, chest pain, irregular pulse, injury from a fall, confusion or neurological signs, persistent vomiting.

Follow-up: NASA flight surgeons assess crew at landing and monitor for the first 30-45 days, with a structured post-flight rehabilitation program [6]. Most readaptation motion sickness clears in 2-3 days [6].
