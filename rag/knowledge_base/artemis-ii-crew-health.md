## Mission facts and status of the results

Artemis II was the first crewed Artemis flight: NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch and CSA (Canadian Space Agency) astronaut Jeremy Hansen flew a lunar flyby in the Orion spacecraft [3][10]. Liftoff was at 6:35 p.m. EDT on 1 April 2026 and splashdown off San Diego at 8:07 p.m. EDT on 10 April 2026, a mission of nearly 10 days [10][13]. Orion reached a maximum distance of 252,756 statute miles from Earth [10].

**Status of health results (checked 13 November 2026):** NASA has described the studies and the post-flight data collection, but no crew health results had been published in the NASA, NTRS, CSA or peer-reviewed sources read for this document. The NASA research update after landing says the science teams are still collecting and analyzing data [3]. The NTRS Artemis II Preliminary Science Report is about lunar science and states that the Human Research Program, the organ chip investigation and the radiation monitor were managed separately and are outside its scope [11]. The NTRS paper "Artemis II Test Flight: Lessons Learned" covers the launch vehicle, Orion and operations, and reports no crew health findings [10]. Doses, motion sickness rates, sleep data and immune results are therefore unknown, and this assistant must not estimate them.

## Health studies on Artemis II

Three Human Research Program studies and the AVATAR organ chip investigation flew on Artemis II, together with a radiation monitoring network [11]; the CSA lists five studies, counting radiation monitoring [12]:
- **ARCHeR (Artemis Research for Crew Health and Readiness)**: wristband actigraphy devices record movement and sleep before, during and after the flight, with cognition, behavior and teamwork data before and after; real-time data support health monitoring and safety [1][2].
- **Immune Biomarkers**: dry saliva blotted on paper in pocket-sized booklets (no refrigeration in Orion) before, during and after the flight, plus liquid saliva and blood before and after, to study immune function and reactivation of dormant viruses [1][2].
- **AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response)**: organ chips with bone marrow cells made from each crewmember's pre-flight blood flew in Orion, the first use of organ chips beyond the Van Allen belts [1]. On flight day 4 NASA reported the payload was operating as expected [8].
- **Artemis II Spaceflight Standard Measures**: blood, urine and saliva for nutrition, cardiovascular and immune status; MRI for eye and brain health; balance, vestibular function, muscle performance and microbiome tests; an assessment of motion sickness symptoms during the flight; data collected from about six months before launch until about a month after return [1][2][9].

During the flight the crew also answered periodic questions about conditions aboard Orion [8].

## Health-related activities during the flight

NASA's mission updates and agenda describe these activities [5][6][7]:
- **Exercise**: daily sessions on Orion's flywheel, a 30-pound cable device used for rowing, squats and deadlifts with loads up to 400 pounds, to keep cardiovascular conditioning [15][7].
- **Emergency medical procedures**: on flight day 3 the crew carried out a CPR and choking-response demonstration [7]. The agenda planned that Wiseman and Glover would check Orion's medical kit, including the thermometer, blood pressure monitor, stethoscope and otoscope [5]. The mission objectives list medical procedures, a radiation shelter and fire response among the emergency procedures to be demonstrated, and medical imagery for private medical conferences [14].
- **Return to gravity**: late in the return trip the crew was to test and evaluate an orthostatic intolerance garment worn under the Orion suit, a lower-body compression garment meant to support blood pressure after microgravity [6]. The plan included body measurements and a fit questionnaire [5].
- **Radiation shelter**: a shelter-building demonstration was planned for flight day 8, but the teams forwent the planned shielding deployment demonstration to prepare the cabin for entry [5][6].
- **Hygiene hardware**: before the apogee raise burn a toilet fault light was resolved [16]; on flight day 4 a wastewater vent line problem meant the crew was told to use backup collection devices if needed, while the toilet stayed operational [8].

None of the NASA flight-day updates read for this document reports a crew medical event. This is not an official statement that none occurred.

## Radiation monitoring and the pre-flight expectation

Orion carried six active Hybrid Electronic Radiation Assessor (HERA) sensors at different cabin locations, each crewmember wore a pocket Crew Active Dosimeter (first use by crew beyond Earth orbit), and four updated German Space Agency M-42 EXT sensors (six times the energy resolution of the Artemis I version, to distinguish heavy ions) were placed around the cabin [1][4]. Orion's systems display a warning and sound an alarm if radiation levels rise; a first threshold prompts closer monitoring and coordination with medical and flight teams, and a higher threshold triggers a recommendation to take shelter [4]. For a solar particle event the crew would add mass around the hottest areas of the cabin by moving stowed equipment [4]. NASA and NOAA watched the Sun around the clock and built models forecasting hazardous solar particles up to 24 hours ahead [4][11].

Before the flight, NASA expected the exposure from galactic cosmic rays and the Van Allen belts to be comparable to a 1-month stay on the ISS, about 5% of an astronaut's career limit; solar events would add to this [4]. This was an expectation, not a measurement: measured crew doses from Artemis II were not found in any source read. See the radiation dose reference and solar particle event documents for the limits and procedures.

## After landing

After splashdown the crew underwent post-mission medical evaluations aboard the recovery ship USS John P. Murtha, then flew to Houston for post-flight reconditioning, medical and human performance evaluations [13][17]. Within a day of splashdown researchers collected the Standard Measures data (blood pressure, heart rate, eye health, motor control) and the crew did a mini obstacle course. Over the next days they completed obstacle courses in spacesuits offloaded to lunar gravity, to learn how soon crews can do mission-critical tasks after a landing without support personnel [3]. Initial data collection for the health studies ended 45 days after splashdown; medical teams will continue to monitor the crew's health throughout their lives [3]. Anonymized data will be available through NASA's Life Sciences Data Archive [3]. The AVATAR chips are being analyzed in a laboratory in Boston by single-cell RNA sequencing against ground controls and crew blood samples, and NASA plans to share early findings at scientific conferences [3].

## How to use this document

- Use it to answer questions about what Artemis II measured and when; do not quote numbers for crew radiation dose, motion sickness, sleep or immune changes, because none had been published.
- For reference values and procedures (space motion sickness, radiation limits, orthostatic intolerance, sleep), use the other documents, which rely on ISS and NASA standard sources.
- Artemis II lasted only about 10 days, so its findings will describe the first days of deep-space flight; applying them to missions of months would need caution (this is an inference, not a NASA statement).
