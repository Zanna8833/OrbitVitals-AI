## Why menstruation is usually suppressed

Pregnancy is contraindicated during spaceflight and much of pre-flight training, which lasts at least 6 months, so effective contraception is essential [1]. Many female astronauts also choose to suppress menstruation [1][2]:
- the US waste and water recovery systems on ISS were not designed to handle menstrual blood, which is why breakthrough bleeding should be minimized [1];
- managing hygiene products in microgravity is a practical burden (mass, volume, waste disposal) [2].

Menstrual suppression is a personal choice made with the flight surgeon; NASA's 2016 review stresses up-to-date education and respect for the astronaut's autonomy [1]. Pre-mission checks include a pregnancy test with urinalysis and a pelvic ultrasound for women [6]. Abnormal uterine bleeding is on NASA's list of conditions a program may need to treat in flight [5].

## Options and their trade-offs

From the 2016 npj Microgravity review [1]:
- **Continuous combined oral contraceptive pill**: the method with the longest spaceflight experience; amenorrhea in up to 80% at 1 year; protects bone density compared with injections, but needs daily intake, can cause breakthrough bleeding, and pill stability over multi-year missions is uncertain. A 3-year exploration mission would need about 1,100 pills. Estrogen side effects include venous thrombosis, migraine and stroke.
- **Levonorgestrel intrauterine device (LNG-IUD)**: amenorrhea in up to 80% at 1 year, licensed for 5 years, no pills to carry and no effect on femur or spine bone density at 2 years. Risks: perforation (1 to 9 per 1,000 insertions) and expulsion (2-10% in the first year). The authors suggest inserting it 1.5-2 years before flight to learn the bleeding pattern, and favor it over implants for long missions.
- **Progestin implant**: lower amenorrhea rates (11% in the first 90 days, about 41% at 3 years).
- **Depot medroxyprogesterone injection**: associated with bone density loss, a problem when spaceflight already causes bone loss.
- **Progestin-only pill**: not recommended for suppression, because only about half of users stop ovulating.

## Venous thrombosis and hormones (2026 NASA guidance)

NASA's 2026 venous thrombosis brief [2]:
- First- and second-generation estrogen-progestin pills increase thrombosis risk **up to 3.2-fold**, and are considered in NASA's in-flight clot risk algorithm.
- A **progesterone-only method (IUD or implant) may be preferred** for suppressing menstrual bleeding in flight because of its lower clot risk.
- Crewmembers with an immediate family member who had an unprovoked clot should consider not using oral estrogen, and crewmembers with a thrombophilia should not use combined pills.
- Jugular vein clots have been found on ISS; facial or eyelid swelling beyond normal space puffiness and neck vein swelling should be reported (see the venous thrombosis document).

The 2016 review lists thrombosis as an estrogen side effect without quantifying it in spaceflight [1]; the 2026 NASA brief is the current reference for clot risk [2].

## Known sex differences in spaceflight

- **Orthostatic intolerance after landing** is more frequent in women, who also showed a greater fall in plasma volume [3] (see the orthostatic intolerance document).
- **SANS / vision changes**: in 25 ISS crewmembers evaluated, signs appeared in 5 of 8 women (62.5%) and 14 of 17 men (82.3%), with milder findings in women [3].
- **Urinary retention**: the odds of in-flight urinary retention are **4.5 times higher in female astronauts**, very different from Earth data; female anatomy and urine splash-back in the absorbent garment also make urinary infection easier [4] (see the urinary document).
- **EVA gloves**: in NASA's glove injury analysis (training and flight events combined), women had about 2.6 times the odds of fingernail delamination (borderline significance); men had a greater risk of any injury overall [8].
- **Radiation**: the career limit of 600 mSv is the same for all ages and sexes; it is calculated with 35-year-old female model parameters, the most sensitive reference [7].

## Notes for the assistant

- Do not suggest starting, stopping or switching hormonal treatment; this is decided with the flight surgeon, ideally long before flight [1][2].
- Report to the flight surgeon: unexpected vaginal bleeding, pelvic pain, missed pills of a suppression regimen, and in a crewmember taking estrogen any neck vein swelling, facial or eyelid swelling beyond normal space puffiness, or headache [2][5].
- When judging a female crewmember's post-landing dizziness or urinary symptoms, remember the higher baseline risk above [3][4].
