## What the risk is

NASA's Human Research Program names a "Risk of Injury from Dynamic Loads": astronauts face factors that raise the chance of injury when the vehicle lands back on Earth. Listed contributors are gravitational transitions, possible disorientation and bone loss [1]. Landing is called out as the phase that creates injury risk, and the most dynamic event of a mission [1][4]. Moving between gravity fields (microgravity, low lunar gravity, back to the spacecraft, then Earth) may leave bones and muscles weaker, which raises injury risk [1]. The formal risk title also covers long-term health effects [2].

## Why current knowledge is limited

NASA's Human System Risk Board states [2]:
- Vehicle standards and design requirements are based on automotive and military environments and only partly tailored to spaceflight, and do not adequately mitigate injury risk in the spaceflight environment.
- Analytical tools are used outside the conditions they were validated for and have been shown to underpredict Soyuz landing injury.
- Injury from standing during dynamic phases of flight is poorly documented, with little data.
- Injury assessment reference values have not been validated because load and injury data from Soyuz landings are limited.

Do not treat any injury threshold as established for crew on a given vehicle: the sources above say the data are limited.

## Injuries seen in Soyuz landings

The NASA Evidence Report on dynamic loads (Rev A, 2021, read in full) says dynamic loads are transient loads lasting less than 500 ms, most likely during launch, launch or pad abort, parachute deployment and landing [6]. Capsule vehicles expose the crew to them on every flight [6]. Soyuz is the main source of real landing data: as of 31 December 2019 it had flown 141 flights with 286 crewmembers, and NASA has recorded landing injuries in US crewmembers systematically since 2003 (70 crewmembers in the ongoing study) [6].

The report's table of known Soyuz abort and landing injuries lists 46 minor and 7 moderate injuries and no severe injuries, with the highest counts in the newest vehicle type (TMA/MS: 36 minor and 5 moderate in 54 flights); minor and moderate injuries are suspected to be underreported, especially for early missions [6]. The report's table of injury incidence by landing type gives any injury in 6.9% of crewmembers in nominal landings, 14.7% in off-nominal landings and 29.6% in hard landings (parachute failure, retrorocket failure, ballistic re-entry), 15.3% overall; the dynamics that caused each injury are mostly not available [6]. The same report states, in its conclusions, that about 10% of crewmembers sustain minor or moderate injuries in nominal landings, whereas NASA's tools predict less than 1%; this figure differs from the 6.9% of the table (probably because it comes from the US crew study), so treat both as approximate [6]. The report does not give a list of injury types for these landings.

## Risk factors named by NASA

- **Vehicle and landing dynamics**: each vehicle has different launch, abort and landing loads, so risk depends strongly on the vehicle [6].
- **Seat and restraints**: they can raise or lower risk depending on how well they limit body movement relative to the seat [6].
- **Spacesuit and helmet**: the suit can hinder the restraints, rigid suit parts can cause point loading, and the helmet can cause blunt impact or overload neck muscles if the neck is not properly supported [6].
- **Age, sex and body size**: men and women differ in strength and geometry, and a one-size helmet is a larger burden on a smaller neck; no injury criteria yet account for differences between men and women [6].
- **Deconditioning after microgravity**: after exposure to microgravity the crew may tolerate dynamic loads less than at the start of the mission, because bone, muscle and the spine change [6]. The Columbia crew survival investigation concluded that inadequate upper-body restraint and protection could be lethal [6].

## Deconditioning and the spine

NASA applies scaling factors to reduce injury tolerance limits after more than 1 month in reduced gravity: 0.86 for the spine, 0.75 for the lower extremities including the hip, and 1.0 for other regions. They are meant to protect crew after up to 6 months in space, and limits for longer missions should be agreed by a panel of medical experts [6]. These are design factors for vehicle engineers, not values a crewmember can measure.

Exposure to microgravity changes the intervertebral discs and the shape of the spine, and astronauts report generalized back pain, likely from spine lengthening or muscle weakness; the discs may adapt in a way that makes them more susceptible to injury at higher g loads, but the report says no research yet addresses injury under dynamic loading for a deconditioned spine [6]. The report notes that exercise devices such as ARED are used on the ISS to mitigate deconditioning [6]. In laboratory impact tests on human volunteers (Mertz et al.), a subject with tensed neck muscles tolerated higher loads than the same subject relaxed [6]; this is a research finding at sub-injurious loads, not an instruction for landing.

## What NASA is doing about it

- Building human body models to characterize how the body is affected in different spaceflight environments, to set vehicle design standards such as seat configuration and restraints used to strap in for landing [1].
- Improving spacesuit design to better protect astronauts from spaceflight forces [1].
- A flight study, Spacecraft Occupant Protection (formal title Spacecraft Occupant Risk), surveys crew about discomfort during re-entry and combines it with spacecraft sensor data, to improve injury prediction models and re-entry protocols [4].

## Standing landings and the Moon

For lunar and Mars landers the crew may have to stand during dynamic phases of flight. Most research involves seated occupants, so very little data exists for standing; 12 crewmembers landed on the Moon standing during Apollo with no complications, but acceleration histories are not available and the landing loads were approximated [6]. If the crew wears an EVA suit during planetary landings, blunt injury from rigid suit elements could occur, and the suit's contribution to injury risk is unquantified [6]. The report also states that the crew will be increasingly deconditioned on Mars missions and extended lunar stays, which will affect injury tolerance [6].

## Readiness to act after landing

The Evidence Report notes that injury during landing may impair or prevent the crew from evacuating the vehicle unassisted [6]. After an Earth landing, recovery teams help the crew exit the vehicle. On the Moon and Mars the crew will have to get out and function autonomously soon after landing [1]. NASA describes the symptoms after return from space as often dizziness, lightheadedness, nausea and being off-balance, lasting for several days [3].

NASA designed tests, published in 2022, to measure how soon after landing crew can do mission-critical tasks [3]:
- A mock capsule exit a few hours after landing: stand up from lying down, unfurl and secure a ladder, climb through a hatch, hand off a survival pack, descend and walk about 25 feet. Posture changes such as head-turning and standing up after lying down were described by the researcher as the hardest part [3].
- A simulated planetary spacewalk about a day later, in a suit connected to the ARGOS gravity offload system tuned to Mars gravity (about three-eighths of Earth's), including climbing, connecting supply lines and moving 30-pound objects [3].

The CIPHER study suite includes "Egress Fitness", which asks astronauts back from missions of up to a year to do the capsule exit and suited tasks, to gauge what they can do after landing on a planetary surface [5]. The sources describe the study designs; they do not give results.
