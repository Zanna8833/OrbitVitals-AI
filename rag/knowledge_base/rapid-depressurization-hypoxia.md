## What a rapid depressurization is

A rapid depressurization ("rapid depress") is an unplanned loss of cabin air above the normal design leakage of the pressure vessel [1]. Causes include micrometeoroid/orbital debris strikes, seal or structural failure, or an open overboard valve. It is one of the four ISS emergency categories, together with fire, toxic atmosphere and medical emergency [1].

ISS cabin pressure is normally 14.7 psia (101.3 kPa) with 21% oxygen, equivalent to sea level [2]. The medical danger of losing pressure is **hypoxia** (falling oxygen partial pressure), then decompression sickness and, at near-vacuum, ebullism (body fluids boiling at about 0.91 psi / 47 mmHg at body temperature) [2]. The Soyuz 11 crew (1971) died when a valve opened at high altitude during entry while they were not wearing pressure suits [2][3].

## Immediate actions on ISS: Warn, Gather, Work

ISS crews memorize their initial response, because Mission Control may be out of contact and **the crew leads the emergency response** [1]:

1. **Warn**: the caution-and-warning system annunciates automatically (or a crewmember/MCC triggers it). Verbally warn crewmates who may be out of earshot. The vehicle automatically closes overboard valves, stops fans and gas introduction, and safes equipment [1].
2. **Gather**: all crew go to the designated gathering point and account for everyone [1].
3. **Calculate reserve time (TRes)**: using a manual pressure gauge (manovacuometer), a watch and nomographs (computers may also do it). TRes is the time until cabin pressure reaches 490 mmHg (9.5 psia), the ISS minimum, slightly below where hypoxia risk begins to rise [1].
4. **Verify the return vehicle is leak tight** (Soyuz / crew vehicle and its adjacent module) [1].
5. **Isolate the leak** by closing hatches segment by segment ("divide and conquer"), recalculating TRes after each closure [1].
6. **Never close a hatch between yourself and your return vehicle**: hatches can jam and pressure difference can make them impossible to reopen [1].
7. **If TRes reaches 10 minutes or less, stop leak hunting**, close off into the last known stable volume, which may mean evacuating into the return vehicle [1].

## Recognizing hypoxia in yourself and crewmates

Hypoxia can be subtle and the affected person often does not notice it; cognitive impairment may prevent self-recognition, so crew are trained to watch each other [2]. Signs listed by NASA [2]:

- Mental changes, sometimes undetectable to the person (poor judgment, slowed thinking).
- Reduced color, night and peripheral vision.
- Fatigue, nausea, headache, dizziness, mood changes.
- Hyperventilation; poor sleep with milder chronic exposure.

Reference points: a sea-level-adapted person may begin to show subtle decrements near arterial PaO2 of 80 mmHg and progress to loss of consciousness around 30 mmHg [2]. All astronauts receive altitude/hypoxia chamber training before flight to learn their own symptoms [2].

## Treatment of hypoxia

1. Give supplemental oxygen immediately: quick-don oxygen masks / portable breathing apparatus (PBA) [1][2].
2. Move the crewmember to a volume with normal pressure if possible.
3. Restrain the crewmember; an impaired crewmember must not work the emergency alone.
4. Once stabilized, monitor SpO2 and mental status, and report to the flight surgeon.
5. Avoid prolonged 100% oxygen beyond need: NASA limits pure-O2 exposure duration because of lung (atelectasis) risk and oxygen-enriched air raises fire risk [2].

Note: below about 2.5 psia (130 mmHg), supplemental oxygen alone may not maintain tissue oxygenation [2]; at that point only a pressure suit or repressurized volume protects the crew.

## Orion and exploration vehicles (single cabin)

On Orion, the cabin is about 2% of the ISS volume, so a leak that would take 6.5 hours to bleed down ISS would take under 10 minutes on Orion [1]. There is no separate lifeboat, so the response is entirely crew protection [1]:

1. Crew immediately don their pressure suits.
2. The vehicle closes overboard valves and adds oxygen to prevent hypoxia, holding cabin pressure at 8 psia for up to one hour to allow suit donning and leak checks.
3. Once crew are suited and connected, the cabin may be allowed to go to vacuum; crew systems support suited crew for up to six days for a return from the Moon [1].

Pressure suits must be worn during launch, entry and other high-risk phases for this reason [3].

## When to contact the flight surgeon / follow-up

After the vehicle is safe, contact Mission Control and the flight surgeon for every crewmember who:
- had hypoxia symptoms or lost consciousness;
- has joint pain, skin mottling, numbness, weakness, confusion or visual problems after the pressure drop (possible decompression sickness, see the decompression sickness document);
- has ear or sinus pain, hearing loss, or chest pain/shortness of breath (barotrauma, possible pneumothorax) [2].

Monitor: SpO2, respiratory rate, heart rate, mental status and symptoms; log the cabin pressure profile (minimum pressure and duration) because it determines DCS risk.
