## Recognition

A seizure causes loss of consciousness and uncontrolled muscle contractions; seizures typically last less than a minute [1]. After a seizure the person is confused for several minutes [1].

Seizure-like collapse can also come from hypoxia, very high CO2, toxic exposure or cardiac arrest. Check the cabin atmosphere (see cabin atmosphere reference and rapid depressurization, toxic atmosphere and CO2 documents) and check for a pulse; if there is no pulse, start CPR (see cardiac arrest document). High CO2 levels can cause severe symptoms and, in extreme cases, death [3]; low oxygen causes loss of consciousness [2].

## Immediate actions (ISS checklist, 2015)

1. **Protect from injury**: prevent the body from hitting hard structures; try to move the patient to the center of a module, away from structures [1].
2. **Never** try to pry open the mouth or put anything in it during a seizure [1].
3. **Diazepam**: prepare 2 mL of diazepam 5 mg/mL (10 mg total) and give it as an intramuscular injection. If the seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, or seizures repeat, repeat the dose after 10 minutes [1].
4. **Pulse oximeter**: apply and record SpO2 [1]. Steps 3 and 4 can run in parallel if more than one Crew Medical Officer is available [1].
5. **Oxygen** via low-flow non-rebreather mask from the Respiratory Support Pack [1].
6. **IV access** [1].

## After the seizure

7. **Targeted exam**: head, limbs, teeth and tongue for injuries; photograph injuries [1].
8. **Call the surgeon** (request a private medical conference) [1].
9. **Phenytoin**: when the patient is awake, coherent and able to swallow, give one capsule of phenytoin by mouth as directed by the surgeon, and a second dose after two hours or as directed [1]. The 2015 kit lists phenytoin extended-release 300 mg capsules [1].
10. **History**: collect information about the event and any medications used; downlink photos [1].

## In-flight specifics

- In microgravity the person does not fall, but limbs and head can strike structures, and the body can drift; restraint and positioning in the open middle of a module is the checklist approach [1].
- Diazepam causes sedation; monitor breathing and SpO2 afterwards.
- Keep each crewmember's photos and data in separate folders labeled with name and GMT day [1].

## When to contact the flight surgeon

- Every seizure: the checklist calls for a private medical conference once the immediate steps are done [1].
- Immediately (while treating) if: the seizure lasts more than 5 minutes or repeats; breathing is inadequate or SpO2 stays low despite oxygen; there is head injury; the patient does not wake up; or several crewmembers have symptoms (suspect the atmosphere).

## Follow-up

- Neurological checks and SpO2 monitoring as directed by the surgeon.
- Record seizure duration, diazepam doses and times, SpO2 values and phenytoin doses for the surgeon [1].
- Review possible causes: head injury, medication changes, sleep deprivation, infection, atmosphere readings.
- Any seizure is a serious event for mission planning; the decision on continued duties or return belongs to the flight surgeon and mission management.
