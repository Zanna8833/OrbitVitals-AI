## Q: What should I do in the weeks before landing to avoid fainting?

Orthostatic intolerance after landing is common: blood volume is lower and the circulation has adapted to microgravity [1]. Countermeasures NASA uses [1][2]:
- Keep up aerobic and resistance exercise until the end of the mission.
- Fluid and salt loading before deorbit, as prescribed by weight; current protocols use about 0.7-1.4 L of salt-water solution.
- Wear the compression garment during entry and landing.
- Use active cooling, and stay recumbent or seated after landing.

ISS crew have daily private medical conferences starting five days before entry and on landing morning [3]. Report any dizziness, nausea or illness during that period. See the orthostatic intolerance document.

## Q: I felt dizzy and nearly fainted when I stood up after landing. What is happening?

After flight your blood volume is lower and your blood pressure control has adapted to microgravity, so standing up can reduce blood flow to the brain. This is orthostatic intolerance [1]. Returning crew have 12-15% less blood volume, along with readaptation motion sickness and unsteady balance [2].

What to do:
1. Lie down or recline immediately, and do not walk without help.
2. Keep the compression garment on.
3. Drink fluids if you are not nauseated, and cool down if you are hot [1][2].
4. Have your pulse, blood pressure and SpO2 checked [2].

Get medical help immediately if you lose consciousness, have chest pain or an irregular pulse, are injured from a fall, or are confused. Most readaptation symptoms clear in days, and flight surgeons monitor crew after landing [2].

## Q: On the Moon after six months in microgravity I feel unstable and dizzy. How long until I adapt?

The brain adapts to microgravity within about 2 weeks. On return to gravity, readaptation takes hours to days, with balance problems and visual inconsistencies [4]. Symptoms can be severe enough to prevent tasks such as recovering from a fall [4]. On landing, most readaptation motion sickness clears in 2-3 days [2]. Partial gravity on the Moon has not been studied the same way, so expect individual variation.

What to do:
- Move slowly, avoid quick head movements, and use handholds.
- Postpone demanding physical tasks if possible.
- Report symptoms that worsen or do not improve over several days.

ISS rules similarly avoid EVAs in the first 72 hours after arrival because moving provokes symptoms during adaptation [5].

## Q: I'm about to start an EVA. What should I check about my health?

- A private medical conference is held within 24 hours before suit donning and within 24 hours after suit removal [3].
- Hydration: plan for at least 240 mL of water per EVA hour in the suit [6].
- Sleep and fatigue: report short sleep or a sleep aid taken the previous night.
- Report symptoms such as headache, nausea, joint or muscle pain, congestion or ear problems, and any new medication.
- Check the cabin atmosphere and that the prebreathe protocol is complete (see the decompression sickness document).
- Early in flight, no EVA is scheduled before 72 hours after arrival, to allow adaptation [5].

During the EVA, report immediately any joint pain, numbness, skin changes, headache, shortness of breath or eye irritation.

## Q: Communications with Earth are down for days. What must I handle myself?

Beyond low Earth orbit, crews must solve more problems themselves with what is on board [7]. The Mars-Earth delay is estimated at 3-23 minutes each way [8].

Handle immediately with the onboard procedures, without waiting for the ground:
- cardiac arrest (CPR);
- severe allergic reaction (epinephrine);
- choking;
- major bleeding;
- fire, toxic atmosphere or depressurization;
- seizure [9][8].

Manage common problems with the kit and procedures: motion sickness, headache, congestion, back pain, minor injuries and sleep problems.

Record everything for later: symptoms, vital signs, times, medications given, and photos. The most medically qualified crewmember decides when ground advice is unavailable [8]. Report all of it when communication returns.

## Q: A medication in the kit is past its expiration date. Is it still safe?

Ask the flight surgeon before taking it. Evidence so far is reassuring for some medications. In a NASA study of nine medications kept on ISS for 550 days, all except melatonin still contained 90-110% of their labeled active ingredient [10]. Low Earth orbit experience has not shown stability problems leading to poor health outcomes [10].

On ISS, medications are restocked before they expire. Exploration missions of 1-5 years will exceed the usual 1-2 year expiration dates, which NASA is still studying [10].
