## Recognition

Barotrauma is injury caused by a change in surrounding pressure that the gas trapped in a body space cannot follow fast enough [1]. Most often it affects the ears and sinuses, but also the teeth and lungs [1]:
- **Ear or sinus squeeze**: the middle ear or a sinus fails to equalize; causes pain, bleeding or irritation of the lining. A ruptured eardrum is possible [1].
- **Dental squeeze**: gas expanding inside a tooth causes pain or a fracture [1].
- **Lung barotrauma**: rapid expansion of gas in the lung can cause a collapsed lung (pneumothorax) or arterial gas embolism [1].

Injury is more common when the trapped gas is at lower pressure than the surroundings (during repressurization), but injuries from the opposite direction also occur [1].

On ISS, ear problems are linked to sinonasal symptoms: in a 2025 review of 71 ISS astronauts, ear clearing and barotrauma accounted for 17 logged events (7 astronauts, 10% of the 71, had a first such event), and astronauts with EVA in their records had on average about 9 more sinonasal records than those without [4]. NASA lists barotrauma and hearing loss among conditions a program may need to treat in flight [5].

## When it happens in spaceflight

Pressure changes occur when crewmembers move between volumes at different pressures, for example from the cabin into an EVA suit, or during airlock depressurization and repressurization [1]. Future lunar missions may involve repeated transfers between a habitat, a pressurized rover and a suit, and unplanned pressure changes can follow vehicle damage [1].

Inside the suit, pressure fluctuations force the crewmember to keep re-equalizing body cavities and sinuses, especially the ears; NASA requires suits to hold pressure within 0.1 psi once a set point is reached and to limit the rate of pressure change to prevent discomfort and barotrauma [3].

Vehicle limits (NASA-STD-3001) [2]:
- For pressure changes larger than 1.0 psi, the cabin pressure must not change faster than **13.5 psi per minute**, to protect ears and lungs during depressurization and repressurization.
- During a commanded pressure change the crew must be able to **pause within 1 psi** and then raise or lower pressure as needed to avoid discomfort and barotrauma [2][1].

Nasal congestion from the headward fluid shift makes equalizing harder (see the nasal congestion document).

## Immediate actions

1. **Pause the pressure change** as soon as ear or sinus pain starts; the system is required to allow a pause within 1 psi [1][2].
2. **Equalize**: NASA prevention relies on gradual, controlled pressure changes and crew training in equalizing techniques such as the Valsalva maneuver [1]. Resume the pressure change slowly only when the ears clear.
3. **Medication per flight surgeon**: NASA lists decongestants and pain medication as part of prevention and care [1] (see the nasal congestion and medical kit documents for what is on board).
4. **Do not continue an EVA preparation** with an ear that will not clear; report it to the EVA team and flight surgeon.
5. **After an EVA or pressure drop**, treat new ear symptoms with caution: ringing in the ears, hearing loss, vertigo, nausea and poor balance are also signs of inner-ear decompression sickness [6]. Straining (Valsalva) can push venous blood and bubbles across a patent foramen ovale [6], so after a decompression ask the flight surgeon before forceful equalizing.

## When to contact the flight surgeon

Urgently for:
- sudden hearing loss, vertigo, ringing in the ears, or nausea after an EVA or pressure change (possible inner-ear decompression sickness) [6];
- chest pain or breathlessness after a pressure change (possible lung barotrauma) [1];
- severe ear pain followed by sudden relief, fluid or blood from the ear (possible ruptured eardrum) [1];
- tooth pain that started with a pressure change (see the dental document).

At the next PMC: blocked ears that persist for more than a day, repeated difficulty clearing before EVAs, or sinus pain with congestion.

## Follow-up and data to monitor

- Log each pressure change (airlock, suit, hatch opening between modules) with any ear or sinus symptoms.
- Hearing assessments are part of NASA's in-flight evaluations (see the hearing and noise document); a change after a barotrauma event should be reported.
- Nasal congestion and decongestant use before planned pressure changes [4].
- Commercial and short-duration flyers: a 2025 scoping review by the Aerospace Medical Association found little published evidence on ear, nose and throat conditions in spaceflight beyond motion sickness and spatial disorientation [7], so screening and clinical judgement of the flight surgeon remain essential.
