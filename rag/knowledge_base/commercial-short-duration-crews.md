## Inspiration4: what three days in orbit did

Inspiration4 carried four civilians for 3 days in low Earth orbit at about 590 km altitude; results were published in Nature in 2024 [1]:
- **Space motion sickness** in 2 of 4 crewmembers (50%), consistent with 50-67% reported for short-duration flights [1].
- **Sleep**: about 6.7 hours per night in flight [1].
- **Cognition**: accuracy was unaffected on 8 of 10 tests, but crewmembers were slower on four tests (including psychomotor vigilance and digit-symbol substitution); one crewmember had a marked performance drop early in flight; post-flight performance matched pre-flight except one test [1].
- **Heart and blood**: heart rate and heart rate variability responses differed between individuals; one crewmember had lower SpO2 in flight [1].
- **Jugular veins**: no clots and no flow anomalies in any crewmember on any flight day, unlike the stagnant or reversed flow that develops later in long ISS missions [1].
- **Immune**: antibody reactivity to Epstein-Barr virus, rhinoviruses and adenovirus rose around flight in some crewmembers without viral symptoms [1].
- **Eye ultrasound** images were below the usability threshold for all four, showing the limits of just-in-time trained operators for some exams [1].
- **Recovery**: almost all measured changes returned to pre-flight values after landing; follow-up ran to about 194 days after return [1].

The authors conclude that short missions did not pose a significant health risk and that minimally trained crews could perform most research procedures, but the study was not designed to judge crew selection or fitness for flight [1].

## When NASA-level selection is not used

NASA notes that rigorous selection should prevent major conditions, because individuals with a substantial risk of significant illness are not certified [2]. If NASA-level selection criteria are not used, programs may need in-mission treatment for [2]:
- major cardiovascular events such as **heart attack and atrial fibrillation**;
- major neurological events such as **seizures and stroke**.

Other points for commercial flyers:
- **Age and clots**: venous thrombosis incidence is about 3 times higher at age 65 or older than at 45-54; NASA notes this may need consideration for commercial flyers who may have other conditions [3].
- **Quarantine**: NASA's Health Stabilization Program starts 14 days before launch; for very short flights (for example less than 1 day) an extensive program may be less critical than for long missions, but contact between quarantined and non-quarantined people must be managed [4].
- **Ear, nose and throat**: a 2025 scoping review for commercial flyers found that motion sickness and spatial disorientation dominate the literature, with little evidence on hearing loss, sinus problems or throat conditions [5].

## Notes for the assistant

- Short missions compress the adaptation phase: space motion sickness, headache and congestion fall in the first days, when the crew is busiest (see the space motion sickness and nasal congestion documents).
- Cognition may be slower even when accuracy is preserved [1]; double-check critical steps.
- For a commercial crewmember with a known medical condition, the pre-flight plan from the flight surgeon takes priority; report chest pain, palpitations, seizure or stroke-like symptoms immediately (see the chest pain and seizure documents).
- Compare each crewmember with their own pre-flight baseline; Inspiration4 showed large individual differences [1].
